Purpose

What the app is for

Syros has a large population of street cats and a small number of people working to care for them. The gap between those two facts is information. Nobody currently knows which cats exist, where they live, which of them have been sterilised, which need help, or where the next effort would do the most good.

There are thousands of people on the island every summer who like cats, carry a camera and walk everywhere. This app turns that attention into a record.

The people it is built for

Visitors experience it as a game. They photograph a cat, find out whether it is one the island already knows, name it if it is new, collect the ones they have met, and see how they rank against other people.

Residents experience it as recognition. The cat somebody has fed for six years gets a name, a page and a history, and the person who feeds it is named on that page.

Families staying for a season, such as those who come to the island with Boundless Life, are a distinct group and matter more than their numbers suggest. They are here for one to three months rather than four days, they range far beyond the harbour, and their children have structured time. They are the group most likely to reach the parts of Syros that short-stay visitors never walk through.

Syros Cats and its volunteers experience none of the above. For your team this is a working record and a tool. It tells you which cats are sterilised, which colonies need attention, which animal somebody has just reported as injured, and what you can put in front of a funder at the end of the year. Nobody on your team is using this app for entertainment, and it has not been designed as though they were.

Two ideas that run through the whole design

The first is citizen science. That phrase describes ordinary people, with no scientific training, collecting real observations that researchers or organisations then use. Birdwatchers counting garden birds on a set weekend each year is the best known example, and the results are used in genuine population studies. This app is the same idea applied to cats: a visitor photographing a cat in an alley is not playing at data collection, they are doing it. That is what makes the app worth a school's time, and it is explained further in section 16.

The second is that raising engagement and money for Syros Cats is an explicit objective of this app, not a side effect. A tourist who has met and named a cat is far more likely to give than one who has read a poster, and the app is deliberately built to convert affection for a specific animal into support for the organisation that cares for it. Sections 15 and 17 are about that directly.

The needs of these groups fit together rather than competing. The public side has to stay effortless, because a visitor who finds it tedious simply stops, and every record your team relies on comes from somebody enjoying themselves. The more enjoyable the app is, the more data you get, the better your reporting becomes, and the more compelling the case for donating. Each part feeds the next.

Naming

What we call it, and what we call the people using it

This is worth settling early, because an app store name is painful to change once people have started recommending it to each other, and because the words we choose have to work in Greek, English and French.

What a good name has to do

  • Work in all three languages. The app is properly trilingual and the name should not be the one part that is not.
  • Be findable. Someone typing “Syros” or “cats” into an app store should find it, which argues for a plain, descriptive name.
  • Be memorable and repeatable. Someone in a café should be able to tell a stranger what to search for, which argues for a distinctive name.
  • Travel to other places. You have told us you are talking to organisations elsewhere about doing something similar. A name that only works on Syros would have to be abandoned the first time that happens, and a name built to travel would not.

Purropolis combines “purr” with “-opolis”, the Greek for city. It has a local accident built into it, because Syros's own capital is Ερμούπολη (Ermoúpoli), meaning “Hermes' city”, so a cat-city named the same way belongs to this island in a way it would belong nowhere else. The joke does not survive translation, since “purr” means nothing in Greek, where purring is γουργουρητό (gourgouritó). Each language would therefore get the same joke remade rather than translated, giving Γατούπολη (Gatoúpoli) in Greek, which follows the Ερμούπολη pattern exactly, and Ronronopolis in French, from the French word ronron.

Our recommendation is to use two names together. Both app stores let you set a name and a separate subtitle, and a different pair for each language. So the app could be called Purropolis with the subtitle “Syros Cat Spotters” underneath it. The distinctive name is what people remember and repeat, and the plain subtitle is what makes it findable.

If the conversations with other organisations are serious, we would lean instead toward “Cat Spotters” as the name, with the place as the subtitle, so that Syros, Paros and anywhere else are all the same app with a local face rather than three separate products. That is a decision about your ambitions rather than about words, and we would like your view.

One name to avoid is “Syros Cats”. That is your name, and an app carrying it would imply that the app is you. That matters if the app ever gets something wrong, and it matters more if it ever operates somewhere you do not.

Spotter or snapper?

You suggested “snapper”, after the colloquial sense of snapping a photograph. It is warmer and more casual than “spotter”, and it lowers the barrier, because anyone can take a snap.

Our recommendation is to keep spotter as the name of the role, and to use snap freely as the verb in marketing and in the app's own encouragement. There are three reasons for that.

  • It describes the actual contribution. The value someone adds is not the photograph, it is recognising which cat it is. A hundred photographs of unidentified cats are worth very little, while a hundred confirmed identifications are a census.
  • It supports the educational side. “Spotter” carries the sense of careful observation, as in birdwatching, which connects directly to the citizen science idea in section 16.
  • It translates into the observing sense in all three languages, whereas “snapper” translates only into “photographer”, which is both generic and wrong about what people are doing.

In short, snapping is what somebody does and spotting is what they become. If you would rather lead with “snapper” we can, but we would want the underlying role to remain “spotter”, because everything the data does depends on identification rather than photography.

People

Who uses it, and what they can do

Nobody needs an account to use the app. A spotter opens it and starts immediately. Accounts exist for two different reasons: to unlock extra features for people who want them, and to give weight to the word of people whose word should carry weight.

Everyone is a spotter. That is the base role, and every other role is added on top of it, so a vet is a spotter who can also record surgery.

RoleHow they get itWhat is different for them
SpotterNothing requiredSpots cats, names new ones, contributes observations, collects and competes
ResidentSigns up themselvesAll of the above, plus claiming a cat, recording that they feed one, and keeping their history across devices
Cat guardianInvited by youOversees an area, its feeding sites and the cats in it; their observations about that area carry weight
Syros Cats volunteerInvited by youRecords field observations, confirms or corrects what spotters report, responds to welfare reports
Syros vetInvited by youRecords sterilisations and vaccinations as definitive, including cats you did not fund
Syros Cats managementInvited by youEverything above, plus colonies, broadcasts and reporting
AdminInvited by youInvites and removes people

Residents can sign up on their own. Nothing about claiming a cat or recording that you feed one requires your permission, and asking for it would put a barrier in front of the most valuable contributors on the island.

Every other role comes only by invitation from you. That covers cat guardians, volunteers, vets, your own management team and administrators. There is no way for somebody to sign up and declare themselves a vet. You send an invitation, the person accepts it, and they hold that role until you remove it. If you remove it, everything they recorded stays in place, correctly attributed to them at the time they recorded it.

Signing in does not move somebody into a different app. It adds features to the app they already have, so a volunteer standing in front of a cat can record what they see without going back to a desk, and a resident keeps their spotting history if they change phones.

Journeys

What using the app actually looks like, role by role

The tables above describe permissions. This section describes experience, which is a more useful thing to react to.

A visitor

They hear about the app from a QR code in a café or from another guest. They install it, choose a language, pick a nickname and are spotting within a minute, with no account and no email address. On a walk they photograph four cats. Three are already known, so they confirm each one and earn points, and one is new, so they name her and earn more. The app asks a single question after each spot, such as whether an ear looked clipped, and they answer it in one tap or skip it. Over a week they build a collection, keep a daily streak alive and appear on the weekly leaderboard. When they get home the app tells them, some weeks later, that a cat they named has been sterilised, and offers them a way to help pay for the next one.

A resident

They sign up, which takes a moment, and claim the cat that lives behind their building, giving it a name and describing where it sleeps. They record that they feed it. Because they see the same cats constantly, the collecting side runs out quickly, so the app shifts to asking them different things: whether a cat nobody has seen since April is still around, and whether an animal that looks thin is unwell. Their observations about their own street carry more weight than a passing visitor's.

A family staying for a season

The parents sign up and the children spot under a shared group. The group has a neighbourhood to survey and a target, and the app shows how much of that area has been covered and what remains. Over a few weeks the family works through the education material, and at the end they can see exactly what their group contributed to the island's record.

A cat guardian

They are invited by you and accept. Their area, its feeding sites and its cats are grouped together in the app, and they can see at a glance which cats have not been seen recently, which have no sterilisation record and which sites need attention. When they walk their round they confirm or correct what spotters have reported. If somebody reports an injured cat in their area, they are among the people who could be told.

A Syros Cats volunteer

They open the app already knowing what they are doing that day. Before a trapping round they pull up a colony and see which cats are unsterilised, where each is usually found, and which feeding site they gather at. In the field they record what happened to each cat. The app works without a signal and syncs when it finds one. They are not expected to review other people's contributions, because the record settles itself, but they can correct anything that looks wrong.

A vet

They record each sterilisation and vaccination as they perform it, taking a few seconds, including for cats that did not come through Syros Cats. Their entries are treated as definitive and cannot be overwritten by anybody else's guess.

Syros Cats management

They use the app mostly for the things nobody else can do. They name colonies, send updates to people who follow a particular cat, pull the figures for a funding application, and look at which parts of the island are under-surveyed or under-sterilised when deciding where the next campaign goes.

The core

Spotting a cat

Somebody photographs a cat. The app compares that photograph against the cats it already knows and asks whether this is one it has met before.

It shows the closest few matches, with a plain explanation for each, such as “the white bib and the notch in the left ear match”. The person either confirms one of them, or says that it is nobody they recognise and gives the new cat a name. New names must be unique across the island, and a button suggests unused ones in whichever language they are using.

The app only compares against cats seen within about 500 metres of where the photograph was taken. That is our estimate of how far a street cat ordinarily ranges, and it does two useful things. It makes the matching more accurate, because the app is not being asked to consider cats from the other side of the island that this one could not plausibly be. It also makes it cheaper, since every comparison costs money, as section 20 explains. If 500 metres is wrong for Syros, tell us, because you will have a much better sense than we do of how far these cats actually roam.

Confirming a known cat earns points, and naming a new one earns more. Points build into streaks, badges and a leaderboard, all of which section 14 explains from first principles.

It works in places with no or limited mobile coverage, which matters because Syros has plenty of those and that is often exactly where the cats are. Photographs, locations and the map itself all work offline, and anything waiting is submitted automatically once the phone finds a connection. The sighting is dated to when the photograph was taken rather than when it was uploaded. It does need a connection at some point, because identifying a cat means comparing the new photograph against the record, and that happens on our side rather than on the phone. In practice somebody can spend a day walking with no signal at all and then have everything identified when they get back to a café.

The record

What the app records about each cat

Everything in this section is our assumption rather than your instruction. We have guessed at what is worth capturing, judging each item against two tests: whether it helps you run your work, and whether it helps the public stay engaged or learn something. We have no way of knowing whether we have guessed correctly, so this is the section where your corrections are worth the most.

Each cat has its own page, showing a photograph, a name, a reference code such as SYR-0042, where it is usually seen, everybody who has ever spotted it, and its history.

Appearance covers colour, pattern and distinguishing features such as a white bib, a notched ear or a kinked tail. These are chosen from lists rather than typed, so that a Greek volunteer, a French tourist and an English one describing the same cat all produce the same searchable record rather than three different words.

Health and care covers whether the cat is sterilised, whether its ear is tipped, what it has been vaccinated against, its sex, its approximate age, whether it is pregnant or nursing, its general condition, and whether it has died.

People connected to the cat are recorded as specific relationships rather than a vague sense of who cares for it, because the relationships are genuinely different from each other.

RelationshipWhat it means
FeederSomebody who regularly provides food to this cat, whether at a station or informally
OwnerSomebody who considers the cat theirs, typically a resident whose cat comes and goes
Temporary carerWhoever has the cat right now while it is off the street, such as Syros Cats during convalescence or a vet during and after an operation
Cat guardianNot a relationship to one cat at all. A guardian oversees an area, its feeding sites and the cats within it

How the cat lives is a single status, because not every cat in the record is living the same kind of life.

StatusMeans
StreetFree-roaming, with nobody regularly providing for it
FedFree-roaming, but fed regularly by a feeder or at a feeding station, with no owner

This distinction matters for more than tidiness. It stops the app inviting people to go and find a cat that is recovering indoors, and it lets you see at a glance how much of the island's cat population has somebody keeping an eye on it, which is a different question from how many have been sterilised and just as useful.

Cats that quietly disappear

Most street cats are never confirmed dead. They simply stop being seen. If the app does nothing about that, the catalogue grows for ever, the island's cat count climbs past anything real, and every percentage calculated against it slowly becomes meaningless.

So the app assumes a lifespan. After three years with no sighting at all, a cat moves automatically to “presumed deceased”. It leaves the active population count and stops appearing as a cat to go and find, while staying fully in the record and the history. If anybody photographs it the next day it returns to its previous status immediately and nothing is lost. On the cat's page the app says “not seen since March 2025” rather than announcing a death, because a cat that has moved and a cat that has died look identical from here.

Two questions for you on this section. First, is three years about right for a Syros street cat, given that fed colony cats often live considerably longer than unmanaged ones? It is a single setting and can be changed at any time. Second, and more importantly: what else would be useful to record that we have not thought of? Anything you currently write down, or wish you could, is worth telling us about, even if it seems minor.

Data quality

How the app builds data you can rely on

This is the part we would most like you to understand, because it is what makes your numbers defensible.

Everybody using the app is collecting observations about cats, including their health, and we want as many of those as possible. The design question is not who is allowed to contribute. It is how the app turns a large number of contributions of very different reliability into one answer that is worth publishing.

A tourist, a resident, a volunteer and a vet can all say something about the same cat, and they are not making the same kind of claim. “I think I saw a clipped ear” and “I sterilised this cat on 14 May” are different in kind. If the app simply let each new contribution overwrite the last, a vet's record could be replaced by a guess, and your figures would be worth nothing to a funder.

So the app keeps every observation rather than replacing one with another. Each is dated, attributed, and usually carries the photograph that prompted it. The cat's status is then worked out from all of them together, and the app always shows how confident it is.

LevelMeansExample
ConfirmedSomebody authoritative said soA vet recorded the operation
LikelySeveral people independently agreeThree spotters have seen a clipped ear
ReportedOne person, unconfirmedA spotter thinks she may be pregnant
UnknownNobody has saidMost cats, at the start

The practical effect is that the data improves on its own as the app is used. Every additional observation moves a cat closer to a settled answer without anybody having to review anything. A cat nobody has looked at closely sits at “unknown”, and a cat that several people have independently reported the same thing about becomes “likely” by itself.

Authority is specific rather than general. A vet's word settles whether a cat has been sterilised and carries no special weight on where it sleeps. A guardian's word about their own area outranks a passing spotter's, and never outranks a vet's on surgery.

Nothing is ever deleted. A correction is added on top and the original stays visible, so the record shows how an answer was reached rather than hiding the disagreement.

One rule is worth stating explicitly, because a great deal rests on it.

A clipped ear tip means the cat has almost certainly been sterilised. No visible clip means very little, because plenty of sterilised cats have no clip, or were photographed from the wrong side.

The app therefore treats a clipped ear as strong evidence on its own, and treats “no clip visible” as no evidence at all rather than as evidence against. Please correct us if that does not match what you see in practice.

Contributions

How spotters help without being asked to do work

The app examines each photograph and can often tell what colour a cat is, what pattern it has, and whether an ear looks clipped. It therefore proposes an answer and the person simply confirms it.

One quick thing: is Agatha's ear clipped?

Yes, clipped  ·  No, looks whole  ·  Can't tell

That is one tap. “Can't tell” is a perfectly good answer and stops the app asking again. The question appears after somebody has earned their points, never before, and only ever one question at a time rather than as a form.

The same approach covers appearance. A spotter confirms the cat's colour, its pattern and its distinguishing marks in the same way, usually just by agreeing with what the app has already worked out. Those details matter more than they look, because they are what lets the app tell two similar cats apart, and what helps to identify unique cats with more reliability.

One safeguard is worth knowing about. When the app suggests an answer and somebody agrees with it, we record that the suggestion came first. Five people agreeing with the same suggestion is weaker evidence than five people noticing the same thing independently, and your sterilisation figure should not treat the two as equal. For obvious things such as coat colour the app simply records its own reading, because it is more reliable than a guess from somebody who has never seen a tortoiseshell before.

Safeguards

Keeping the record clean, and keeping it about Syros

An app that lets anybody add content needs protection against misuse, and against ordinary honest mistakes. Some of this is already built, and some of it we are proposing.

Only on Syros

The app is for the cats of Syros, and it should not be possible to fill it with cats from anywhere else, whether mischievously or by accident. So two rules apply.

  • Somebody outside Syros cannot add or change anything. They can look at the catalogue and read about the cats, but adding a sighting, naming a cat or editing a record all require being on the island.
  • A cat cannot be added at a location outside Syros. Even from a phone on the island, a sighting placed somewhere off the island is refused.

This also protects your figures, since a catalogue containing cats from three countries would make every island statistic meaningless.

Already built

  • Offensive names are rejected. Cat names, nicknames and usernames are all checked against a list of offensive words in English, Greek and French. The check is deliberately hard to sidestep, so replacing letters with numbers or writing a Greek word in Latin characters does not get around it.
  • Photographs without a cat in them go no further. The app checks each photograph and, if there is no cat in it, tells the person so and stops there. Nothing is added to the catalogue, no cat is created and no points are earned.
  • Photographs are checked before they are accepted, so that damaged files, or files that are not really images at all, are refused.
  • There are limits on how often the app can be used in quick succession, so that nobody can flood it automatically.

Worth adding, in our view

  • A “report an issue” button on every cat and every photograph. No automatic check catches everything, and when something does get through, the people who see it first will be your users. A single tap that flags it for your team to look at is inexpensive to build and is the difference between an embarrassment lasting an hour and lasting a month.
  • A simple queue for your team showing what has been reported, with the ability to hide a photograph or rename a cat.

A question for you. Who should receive these reports, and how quickly would you want to know? A photograph that somebody finds offensive is not an emergency in the way an injured cat is, but it is the kind of thing that reflects on your name rather than ours.

Welfare

Reporting a cat in trouble

This is kept separate from everything else in the app, is always available, and is never buried. Somebody who finds a cat in trouble takes a photograph, confirms the location, and chooses between “needs attention” and “urgent”.

The step where systems like this usually fail is the middle one, where a report is filed and nothing visibly happens. So the report has to reach a person, and that person has to be able to mark it as seen, being dealt with, or resolved. The person who made the report is then told what happened, which costs almost nothing and is the difference between somebody reporting the next cat and not bothering.

We have deliberately not decided who gets told, because we do not know how you work. Options include everybody in your team, volunteers within a certain distance, the guardian for that area, or a single person on duty who then decides. There is also a real question about whether an urgent report should interrupt somebody's evening at all, or wait until morning. Please tell us what would actually work, including what should happen to an urgent report at two in the morning, and whether other users should be able to see that a report exists or whether that should stay private to the person who made it and your team.

Places

Colonies and the feeding network

Colonies. The app groups cats by where they are actually seen, and you give the groups names. That way the names match the colonies you already talk about, and nothing has to be redrawn every time a cat moves. All of the reporting is grouped by colony, because that is the unit you plan in.

Feeding sites. These are the places where cats are fed, whether fixed stations or informal spots, along with which colony each one serves and what condition it is in. Cats gather predictably where they are fed, so this is also a map of where you can reliably find the cats you need to trap.

Feeders. These are the people who provision those sites. You can record a feeder's name and contact details with their consent, recorded properly and withdrawable at any time.

A question we cannot answer ourselves. Should feeding sites and feeders be visible to the public, or only to you and your volunteers?

Two arguments for keeping them private have been put to us. People who feed street cats sometimes face hostility from neighbours, and publishing their locations would hand that over. Known feeding sites can also attract abandonment, where people leave unwanted cats and kittens in places they know cats get fed. We genuinely do not know how real either risk is on Syros, and you will. There is a good argument the other way too, since a visible feeding map helps people understand that the colonies are being looked after.

Your team

What the app can do for your team

You will go on running most of your work the way you run it now, and the app does not ask you to change that. What it can do is make some of it easier, using information it already holds, so that nothing here asks anybody to type something twice. Take up as much or as little of it as suits you, whenever it suits you.

The data settles itself. You do not have to review or approve what spotters report. As more people observe the same cat the app becomes more certain on its own, and your volunteers only need to get involved where it matters, either by correcting something that looks wrong or by recording something only they can know, such as a clinic visit. If nobody from your team ever opened the app, the record would still improve every week.

Trapping rounds. When you choose a colony, the app can already tell you which cats are not sterilised, where each is usually seen, which feeding site they use, and when each was last seen. That is most of a target list, assembled without anybody compiling it.

What a cat has cost. Because vet visits are recorded against the cat they were for, the cost per cat adds up on its own. That is the figure funders ask for, and it is what makes a claim such as “€60 sterilises a cat” a fact rather than an estimate.

Institutional memory. Small organisations lose a great deal when a long-standing volunteer moves away. Anything recorded here survives that, which is rarely the reason anybody adopts a system and is often the biggest benefit two years later.

Two honest cautions. Every feature here asks somebody to record something, so each one has to save more time than it costs. If it does not, please do not use it, and tell us. And if any of your work does come to rest on this app, you should be able to get everything out of it in a usable form at any time and carry on without it. We would rather write that down now than have you discover it later.

Impact

Reporting what you have achieved

Every figure the app produces carries its own confidence, and says plainly what each part means.

142 cats known on Syros

89 confirmed sterilised, by a vet or by your own records
23 probably sterilised, meaning a clipped ear seen by several people but not yet confirmed
30 with no information either way

Because every observation is dated and attributed, the app can answer a question such as “how many cats did we sterilise between January and December” rather than merely asserting a number. That can be exported, with the method stated, for funding applications and annual reports.

For your own planning, the app can show sterilisation coverage colony by colony, which colonies are growing, which have no feeder, and which parts of Syros nobody has ever photographed. That last one is both a gap in your data and something the app can actively send spotters to fill.

For press and supporters, it can flag milestones worth telling people about, such as the hundredth cat or a colony reaching full coverage.

Counting every sterilisation on the island, not only yours

We would like to offer the island's vets their own way in. A vet with an account could record every cat they sterilise, along with where it lives, whether or not that cat came through Syros Cats.

That turns your reporting from “cats we have sterilised” into “cats on Syros that are sterilised”, which is a far stronger thing to put in front of a funder or a municipality, because it describes the actual state of the island rather than the scale of one organisation's activity. It also stops your volunteers being sent to trap cats that a private owner had done last year. The cost to a vet is a few seconds per operation, and the resulting picture is one that nobody currently has.

Engagement

Points, badges and streaks, and why an app like this needs them

The problem these solve is simple. Nobody is paid to use this app, and nothing obliges anybody to keep going. Left alone, most people would photograph one cat, enjoy it, and never open the app again. Everything below exists to turn a single pleasant moment into a habit that lasts a week, or a season.

IdeaWhat it isWhat it does for you
PointsA number that goes up each time somebody does something useful, such as recognising a known cat or finding a new one.Tells people immediately that what they just did mattered. Also lets us steer effort, because things you need more of can be made worth more.
BadgesSmall awards for reaching a milestone, such as meeting ten cats or being the first to find one in a particular neighbourhood.Gives people goals beyond the next photograph, and a reason to go somewhere they otherwise would not.
StreaksA count of consecutive days on which somebody has spotted at least one cat.The single strongest reason people open an app again the next day, because nobody likes to break a run.

The important design principle, and the reason this section matters to you, is that whatever earns points is what people will go and do. So the points must be pointed at what you actually need. Recognising a cat that is already in the catalogue has to be worth nearly as much as naming a new one, or people will keep declaring cats to be new, and every duplicate makes your island count, your sterilisation percentage and your trapping list wrong. Cats nobody has checked on for months, health details nobody has confirmed, and neighbourhoods nobody has surveyed can all be made worth more, with the reason shown. Used well, the scoring system quietly becomes your task list.

Reach

Getting the island mapped, and keeping people coming back

The problem to solve

Collecting cats is a game that ends. Once somebody has met the cats near them, the app has nothing left to ask, which takes a few days for a visitor and happens sooner and permanently for a resident. So the app needs reasons to open it that are not “find a new cat”.

Fortunately the best of those reasons is something you need anyway.

Checking on cats nobody has seen lately. A cat unseen for two months is a question, and one unseen for six months is a welfare concern. So the app asks: “Nobody has seen Kyra since April. She is usually near the fish market.” That never runs out, it is genuinely useful to you, and it turns the app from a collection somebody finishes into a patch they look after. If we get one thing right for engagement, it should be this.

Telling people what happened to their cats. A message some weeks later saying that Agatha, the cat somebody named in June, has been sterilised is the only thing that works after they have gone home, and it is the whole of the relationship after a trip ends.

Going where nobody has been. For anybody staying long enough to be systematic, a map showing unsurveyed areas turns a walk into a contribution.

Who actually maps the island

Everybody does, and the app depends on that. Residents know their own streets better than any visitor ever will and see the same cats across years. Visitors arrive in large numbers and cover the harbour and the town thoroughly. Neither group, on its own, reaches the parts of Syros that nobody walks through on a short holiday.

That is where longer-staying families, such as those with Boundless Life, are likely to matter most. A family here for one to three months, with children who have structured time and no fixed itinerary, is far more likely to range across Ano Syros, Vari, Galissas and Kini than somebody here for four days near the port. They are not a substitute for residents or visitors, but they are the channel most likely to fill the gaps that the other two leave. Because those groups arrive continuously, each intake is a fresh wave of contributors rather than a one-off push.

Things we would propose doing

  • A coverage map, showing where nobody has looked, so that a walk becomes a contribution.
  • QR codes in cafés, hotels and on ferries, so that the app is discoverable at the moment somebody is looking at a cat. This is cheap, and probably the highest-yield item on the list.
  • Cat-friendly business partners, meaning venues that display the app and appear in it.
  • Telling visitors what happened next, long after they have gone home.
  • Schools and organised groups, which section 16 covers properly.

Teaching

What the app teaches, and the citizen science it makes possible

The app has thousands of people's attention for a week each, at the exact moment they are looking at a street cat. That is a rare opportunity to change what people understand and do, and we would rather use it than fill it with trivia.

Everything below is a concrete proposal. Please cut it, correct it and add to it, because the substance is your expertise rather than ours.

Real science, done by citizen scientists, including children

This is the idea we would most like to build the educational side around, because it is what makes the app worth a school's or a family's time rather than merely enjoyable.

Somebody using this app is not pretending to be a scientist. They are doing the actual work: observing an animal carefully, recording what they see, distinguishing one individual from another, noticing that their own evidence can be wrong, and contributing to a dataset that a real organisation uses to make real decisions. That is what field biology is, and very few people ever get to do it.

What that offers a programme such as Boundless Life is a genuine field study running for the length of a stay, with a defined territory, a real question, data the users collect themselves, and a visible result at the end. It is not a project about cats. It is a contribution to an island's animal welfare programme that would not have happened without them, and one they can point at afterwards.

It also teaches the harder lesson underneath, which is that evidence has different strengths, that one person's sighting is weaker than three, and that “I am not sure” is a legitimate and valuable answer. The app is built on exactly that principle, so users learn it by using the thing rather than by being told.

In practice, a group of users, such as a Boundless Life cohort, can be given a neighbourhood to survey and a target number of cats, along with their own view of what they have contributed.

How the app would actually do that. A group is created with a joining code, and anybody who enters that code has their spotting counted toward the group as well as to themselves. Because every sighting already carries a location, a date and a spotter, the app can then work out and display the group's own figures without anybody compiling anything: how many cats the group has found, how many of those were previously unknown, how much of their assigned area now has coverage and how much remains, and what proportion of the island's total record the group is responsible for. At the end of a stay that becomes a single summary page the group can keep, and Syros Cats can add a note to it saying what was done with the information, such as which colonies were subsequently visited or how many of the cats they found have since been sterilised.

How the teaching reaches people

None of this is delivered as a course somebody has to sit down and work through, because almost nobody would. It is delivered as short pieces attached to the moment where each one makes sense: the explanation of an ear tip when somebody photographs a clipped ear, the arithmetic of an unsterilised colony when they meet a kitten, and the advice about approaching a cat the first time they get close to one. The material below is organised into strands for clarity, but a user simply encounters the right piece at the right time. Anybody who wants to read it all in one go, such as a teacher preparing a session, can do so from a single page.

The feeding nudge

This is the most valuable thing the app can say, at the only moment it will land.

Where it appears. It appears in the spotting flow, on the screen where somebody has just photographed a cat and is waiting a few seconds for the app to identify it. It is not a pop-up, it does not appear on every spot, and it never appears before somebody has had their result. It also appears quietly on a cat's own page beneath its details, and once during the app's introduction.

Thinking of sharing your lunch?

We would gently ask you not to. Here is why, and it takes thirty seconds.

The explanation that follows is brief and does not lecture.

  • Feeding by visitors creates a problem that arrives in winter. This is the part visitors never see. A colony fed generously by tourists through July and August comes to rely on an abundant and easy food source. In October that food source gets on a ferry and goes home. The cats do not, and the island cannot support the numbers that a generous summer encouraged. The animals suffer for it in the months when nobody is watching.
  • Where colonies are fed properly, it is done consistently. Syros Cats and local feeders provide for a number of colonies with the right food, in the right places, all year round rather than only in season. Where a colony has no feeder at all, adding an unpredictable summer food supply makes things worse rather than better, for the same reason.
  • The wrong food makes cats ill. Bread, milk, cured meat and fish bones all do harm.
  • Scattered food draws cats towards roads and creates friction with neighbours, who then want the colony removed altogether.

Critically, it ends with something to do instead. The message closes with a line such as “Syros Cats feeds colonies properly, all year round. €1 covers a cat for a day.” A nudge that only says “do not” is a nudge people ignore, whereas one that redirects the impulse gives the person a way to act on exactly the feeling that made them reach for the sandwich.

We think this may be the single highest-impact feature in the whole app, and it costs almost nothing to build.

Facts, in the seconds people are already waiting

Identifying a cat takes a few seconds, during which the screen would otherwise show nothing but a loading indicator. We would use that moment to show a single short fact, chosen where possible to be about the cat that has just been photographed, because a fact about the animal in front of somebody is the version they remember. Examples of the kind of thing we mean are below.

  • Almost every tortoiseshell is female, because the gene for orange sits on the X chromosome.
  • That notch in her ear is not an injury. It is how vets mark a cat that has been sterilised, done under anaesthetic so she never feels it.
  • White cats with blue eyes are often deaf, which is why this one did not run when you approached.
  • Cats have a scent map of the island that we cannot see. He knows exactly where he is.
  • A cat's greeting is the upright tail. The slow blink means she trusts you.

Strand 3 in full: why sterilisation works

Most people arrive with one of two instincts, either that something should be done about the number of cats, or that nothing should be done to them at all. The same explanation addresses both, and it is worth giving properly.

  • The arithmetic. A single unsterilised female and her surviving descendants can become dozens of cats within a few years, because cats can have two or three litters a year and their daughters begin breeding within months. Shown as a growing figure rather than as a claim, this does the persuading on its own. It also explains that the situation on the island is nobody's fault, because it is simply what cats do unless somebody intervenes.
  • Why removing cats fails. If cats are taken away from a place that has food and shelter, other cats move in from the surrounding area and breed up to fill it, so the numbers return within a season. This is well established, and it is the single most common misunderstanding, including among people who mean well. Sterilising the colony instead leaves the same cats in place, holding their territory, no longer breeding, and gradually declining as they age.
  • What actually happens to the cat. It is trapped humanely, sterilised under anaesthetic by a vet, has the tip of one ear neatly removed while it is asleep so that everybody can see at a glance that it has been done, is allowed to recover, and is then returned to the exact place it came from. That last part matters, because the cat goes home to the territory and the colony it knows.
  • Why an ear tip is a good thing to see. It means the cat will not have kittens, will not fight over mating, will roam less and will live longer. A clipped ear is a cat that somebody has already helped.
  • What it costs and what it buys. The link between a single donation of €60 and a cat that will not have four more litters, each of which would have needed the same again.

Cat jokes

Sponsoring a cat in extended care

We had considered letting somebody sponsor any individual street cat, and we would advise against it. A sponsor paying monthly for a cat that is spotted twice a year, with nothing new to report in between, will conclude that they have been taken for granted, and they would be right.

What does work is sponsoring the cats that are genuinely with you, in extended care. A cat convalescing at Syros Cats for months after an amputation has a real story, a real and continuing cost, and something new to say every week. That is a sponsorship worth having on both sides.

How it would work. A small number of featured cats in long-term care would each have their own page, describing what happened, what treatment they are having, what the care costs and how they are recovering. People could contribute to that cat's care, or fund a specific operation. When the cat recovers, everybody who helped is told and sees the outcome they paid for. This is the strongest fundraising product in the app, and it is honest, because the cost is genuinely ongoing and there is genuinely something to report.

Other ways to give

  • Sponsoring a sterilisation, which is specific and completable, because somebody pays for a named cat's operation and is later told that it happened. Since €50 to €60 is a decision rather than an impulse, letting several people co-fund one cat matters. A page showing “Agatha's sterilisation: €38 of €60, four supporters” lets somebody give €10 and still be part of a specific outcome.
  • Giving it as a gift, such as a sterilisation in somebody's name, which works at Christmas and for gifts made in memory of someone.
  • Sharing and volunteering, for the people who have time rather than money.
  • Showing where it went, by tying every euro back to cats in the record.

Adoption is also possible through the app, though we would expect it to be rare enough that it should not shape the design.

Donations earn points, weighted sensibly. Giving is a genuine contribution and the app should recognise it. The weighting needs choosing with some care, so that money supplements effort rather than replacing it and nobody can simply buy the top of the leaderboard. We would suggest that donation points count towards badges and recognition while sitting on their own footing in the rankings, and we would like your view.

Two practical constraints. Apple and Google take a substantial share of anything bought inside an app, and having charitable donations treated correctly requires following their rules precisely, which usually means the donation happens outside the app itself. Done carelessly, this gets an app rejected from the stores. Separately, you would need to tell us how donations reach you and what receipts or acknowledgements Greek charity rules require.

Privacy

Looking after people's data

The app is designed to know as little about people as it can. Spotters are anonymous, with no name, no email address and no account, and that is a deliberate choice rather than an oversight.

The design objective is to collect as little personal data as possible. The less the app holds, the fewer data protection obligations attach to it and the less there is for anybody to lose. Where personal data is genuinely necessary, the app is built to help you meet those obligations, with a stated purpose for each item, real deletion when somebody asks, retention limits, and access confined to the people who need it.

The personal data the app would hold is limited to the following: the addresses of cat guardians, and optionally their names and contact details; the names and contact details of feeders, with their consent; and accounts for volunteers, vets and your own team. None of it appears anywhere in the public app, which shows only chosen display names.

Who is responsible under GDPR

You asked a good question about this, and it deserves a direct answer. Fair Shake would be providing and operating this app for you as a service, which affects how responsibility is divided.

Data protection law distinguishes between the organisation that decides why personal data is collected, called the controller, and the organisation that handles it on the first one's instructions, called the processor. The controller carries most of the legal obligations, and the processor carries a narrower but real set of its own.

Most likely position
Syros CatsController for the data collected for your work, including guardians, feeders and volunteers. You decide why those records exist and what they are used for.
Fair ShakeProcessor, operating the app on your instructions, plus controller in its own right for the small amount of data that exists only to run the service itself.

In practice that means a written agreement between us, setting out what Fair Shake may and may not do with the data, the security expected, how we help you respond if somebody asks for their data or asks for it to be deleted, how quickly we tell you about any breach, and which suppliers sit behind the service. It also means the data should be stored in the European Union, which the suppliers we propose all support.

We are not lawyers, and this is our understanding rather than legal advice. The division above is the usual one for an arrangement like this, but it is worth confirming, particularly because Fair Shake is making a good many of the design decisions about what gets collected, which can affect the analysis. The concrete step is a short written agreement between the two organisations, and it is much easier to put in place before the app launches than afterwards.

Where cats live

The app shows where each cat is usually seen. For most cats that is harmless and useful, but poisoning incidents are not unknown in Greece, and apps that record wildlife routinely blur the locations of animals that could be harmed by people knowing where they are.

We would therefore propose showing only an approximate location to the anonymous public for any cat you flag as at risk. The exact location would remain visible to Syros Cats, to your volunteers, to the cat's guardian and feeders, and to any signed-in user you choose to trust. The blurring applies only to the anonymous public view, so it never gets in the way of the people who might need to find the animal.

Your call on two things. Whether you want this at all, and which cats or areas it should apply to. It is also worth your view that photographs taken in public sometimes include people in the background, and that children use apps like this. Both are manageable, and both are much easier handled by choice than by complaint.

Recognition

Telling one cat from another

The app compares each new photograph against cats it already knows. This matters more than it might appear, because if the same cat gets registered three times then every number in section 13 is wrong, including the island count, the sterilisation proportion, and the list of cats you think still need trapping.

Recognition therefore gets more attention than a photo-matching feature normally would. The app compares a new photograph only against cats seen within about 500 metres of where it was taken, which is our estimate of how far a street cat ordinarily ranges. That makes the comparison both more accurate and considerably cheaper than considering every cat on the island, and it is a number we would want you to sanity-check.

The accuracy of the matching is measured against sightings that people have already confirmed, since every confirmed sighting is effectively somebody saying that two photographs show the same cat, which is exactly what is needed to test it.

One honest limitation. A great many Greek street cats are tabbies who genuinely look alike, and some pairs will defeat any system. The app is designed to say “I am not sure” clearly rather than to guess, and to let a person settle it.

Later

Souvenirs, as a possible future opportunity

This is not part of the first release, and not part of the version after it. We are recording it here because it could become a useful income stream once the app is established, and because it is worth knowing that it is possible while other decisions are being made. Nothing below needs a decision now.

Somebody who has spent a week meeting the cats of Syros forms a genuine attachment to specific animals. At the moment they leave they want something to take with them, and there is nothing to give them.

The idea is to let people order a physical keepsake showing a cat they photographed, printed and posted to their home address by a printing partner. That could be a magnet, a mug, a print, a tote bag or a set of postcards. It could also include a calendar, published each autumn, which is the classic charity fundraiser and would assemble itself from a year of photographs.

Printing partners of this kind charge a base cost per item and let the seller set the retail price, so the difference belongs to the seller. A mug costing around €9 to print and post within the European Union can sell for €22 to somebody who wants a picture of a cat they met, so a margin of roughly half the retail price is realistic, with calendars better still.

Several things would need thinking through before doing it. There is the question of whose photograph it is, where the simplest and fairest answer is that people can only order souvenirs of photographs they took themselves. There is the question of whether phone photographs print well enough, which they do for small items and not for large ones. There is the choice of partner, where the requirements are production inside the European Union, automated ordering and help with sales tax. And there is the question of whether you would want to be the seller, keeping both the margin and the administration, or let a partner run it and share the revenue.

The reason this is a later question is that a souvenir shop is a small commercial operation, with returns, complaints and postal mishaps attached, and it should never become something that consumes your time. It is worth doing only once the app itself is working and there is somebody to hand the day-to-day handling to.

Boundaries

What we are deliberately not building

These are stated so that expectations are clear.
