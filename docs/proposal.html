Purpose

What the app is for

Syros has a large population of street cats and a small number of people working to care for them. The gap between those two facts is information: which cats exist, where they live, which have been sterilised, which need help, and where the next effort would do the most good.

There are thousands of people on the island every summer who like cats, carry a camera, and walk everywhere. The app turns that attention into a record.

For a visitor, it's a game: photograph a cat, find out whether it's one the island already knows, name it if it's new, collect the ones you've met, and see how you rank.

For a resident, it's recognition: the cat you've fed for six years gets a name, a page and a history, and you're on it.

For Syros Cats and its volunteers, it is none of those things. It is a working record and a tool: which cats are sterilised, which colonies need attention, which animal somebody has just reported as injured, and what you can put in front of a funder at the end of the year. Nobody on your team is using this app for entertainment, and it is not designed as though they were.

The app therefore serves two audiences with opposite needs. For the public it has to be effortless, or they stop and there is no data at all. For your team it has to be dependable, precise and quick to work with. Where the two pull against each other, we keep the public side effortless, because everything your team gets out of the app depends on the public putting things into it.

Naming

What we call it, and what we call the people using it

Worth settling early, because an app store name is painful to change once people have started recommending it to each other, and because the words we choose have to work in Greek, English and French.

What a good name has to do

  • Work in all three languages. The app is properly trilingual and the name shouldn't be the one part that isn't.
  • Be findable. Someone typing “Syros” or “cats” into an app store should find it. This argues for a plain, descriptive name.
  • Be memorable and repeatable. Someone in a café should be able to tell a stranger what to search for. This argues for a distinctive name.
  • Survive success. If this works on Syros it could work on Paros or Naxos. A name with “Syros” in it has to be replaced the first time that happens.

Purropolis is “purr” plus “-opolis”, the Greek for city. It has a local accident built into it: Syros's own capital is Ερμούπολη (Ermoúpoli), “Hermes' city”, so a cat-city named the same way sits naturally on this island in a way it would nowhere else. The pun does not survive translation, since “purr” means nothing in Greek, where purring is γουργουρητό (gourgouritó). So each language gets the same joke remade rather than translated: Γατούπολη (Gatoúpoli) in Greek, which follows the Ερμούπολη pattern exactly, and Ronronopolis in French, from ronron.

Our recommendation is to use both. Both app stores let you set a name and a separate subtitle, and a different pair for each language. So the name can be Purropolis (Γατούπολη / Ronronopolis) with the subtitle “Syros cat spotters” underneath it. The distinctive name is what people remember and repeat; the plain subtitle is what makes it findable. Nothing has to be given up.

One name to avoid: “Syros Cats”. That is your name, and an app carrying it would imply the app is you, which matters if it ever gets something wrong, and matters more if it ever operates somewhere you don't.

Spotter or snapper?

You suggested “snapper”, after the colloquial sense of snapping a photo. It is warmer and more casual than “spotter”, and it lowers the barrier: anyone can take a snap.

Our recommendation is to keep spotter as the name of the role, and use snap freely as the verb in marketing and in the app's own encouragement. Three reasons.

  • It describes the actual contribution. The value someone adds is not the photograph, it is recognising which cat it is. A hundred snaps of unidentified cats are worth very little; a hundred confirmed identifications are a census.
  • It supports the educational side. “Spotter” carries the sense of careful observation, as in birdwatching. That connects directly to the citizen science idea in section 14, which is what makes the app worth a school's or a family's time.
  • It translates into the observing sense in all three languages, where “snapper” translates only into “photographer”, which is both generic and wrong about what people are doing.

So: “snap” is what you do, “spotter” is what you become. If you would rather lead with snapper we can, but we would want the role to stay “spotter” underneath, because everything the data does depends on identification rather than photography.

People

Who uses it, and what they can do

Nobody needs an account to use the app. A spotter opens it and starts. Accounts exist only for people whose word needs to carry extra weight.

Everyone is a spotter. That is the base role, and every other role is added on top of it, so a vet is a spotter who can also record surgery. We have deliberately not called it “visitor”, because a good many of the best spotters live here.

RoleWhoWhat's different for them
SpotterAnyoneSpots cats, names new ones, contributes observations, collects and competes
ResidentAnyone who lives hereAll of the above, plus claiming a cat as theirs
Cat guardianA resident you've endorsedTheir word about their cats' condition carries weight; you hold their contact details

Syros vetInvited by youRecords sterilisations and vaccinations as definitive, including cats you did not fund

AdminYou decideInvites and removes people

Invitations come from you. There is no way to sign up and declare yourself a vet. You send an invitation, the person accepts, and they hold that role until you remove it. If you remove it, everything they recorded stays, correctly attributed to them, at the time they recorded it.

Signing in doesn't put someone into a different app. It adds controls to the app they already have, so a volunteer standing in front of a cat can record what they see without going back to a desk.

The core

Spotting a cat

Someone photographs a cat. The app compares that photograph against the cats it already knows and asks: have we met this one before?

It shows the closest few matches with a plain-English reason for each, such as “the white bib and the notch in the left ear match”, and the person confirms one, or says it's nobody they recognise and gives the new cat a name. New names must be unique across the island, and a button suggests unused ones in whichever language they're using.

Confirming a known cat earns points. Naming a new one earns more. Points build streaks, badges and a leaderboard, which section 12 explains from first principles.

It all works with no signal. Syros has plenty of places with no or limited mobile coverage, and that's exactly where the cats are. Photographs, locations and the map itself work offline; anything waiting is submitted automatically when the phone finds a connection, and the sighting is dated to when the photograph was taken, not when it eventually uploaded.

The record

What the app records about each cat

Everything in this section is our assumption, not your instruction. We have guessed at what is worth capturing, judging each item against two tests: does it help you run your work, and does it help the public stay engaged or learn something. We have no way of knowing whether we've guessed right. This is the section where your corrections are worth the most.

Each cat has a page: photograph, name, a code like SYR-0042, where it's usually seen, everyone who's ever spotted it, and its history.

Appearance, meaning colour, pattern, and distinguishing features like a white bib, a notched ear or a kinked tail. Chosen from lists rather than typed, so a Greek volunteer, a French tourist and an English one describing the same cat produce the same searchable record rather than three different words. It's also how the app can support the way people actually talk, as in “the tortoiseshell by the bakery” rather than “SYR-0042”.

Health and care, meaning sterilised, ear-tipped, vaccinated, sex, age, pregnant or nursing, general condition, and deceased.

Who looks after it, meaning its guardian, if it has one, and where it lives.

How the cat lives. A simple status, because not every cat in the record is living the same kind of life:

StatusMeans
StreetFree-roaming, no regular carer
FedFree-roaming, but fed regularly by a guardian or at a feeding station, with no owner
In careTemporarily off the street, recovering from surgery, being treated, or with a carer
DeceasedConfirmed, or presumed after a long absence (below)

This matters for more than tidiness. It stops the app inviting people to go and find a cat that's recovering indoors, and it lets you see at a glance how much of the island's population has someone keeping an eye on it, which is a different question from how much has been sterilised, and just as useful.

Cats that quietly disappear

Most street cats are never confirmed dead. They simply stop being seen. If the app does nothing about that, the catalogue grows for ever, the island's cat count climbs past anything real, and every percentage calculated against it slowly becomes meaningless.

So the app assumes a lifespan. After a set period with no sighting at all, a cat moves automatically to presumed deceased: it leaves the active population count, stops appearing as a cat to go and find, and stays fully in the record and the history. If anyone photographs it the next day, it returns to its previous status immediately and nothing is lost.

You need to set that period. Street cats on an island like this are commonly said to live a few years, while cats in a managed and fed colony often live much longer, so the right number is a judgement about Syros rather than a fact we can look up. It is one setting and can be changed at any time. We would also suggest showing “not seen since March 2025” on the cat's page rather than announcing a death, since a cat that has moved and a cat that has died look identical from here.

Data quality

How the app builds data you can rely on

This is the part we'd most like you to understand, because it's what makes your numbers defensible.

Everyone using the app is collecting observations about cats, including their health, and we want as many of those as possible. The design question is not who is allowed to contribute. It is how the app turns a large number of contributions of very different reliability into one answer that is worth publishing.

A tourist, a resident, a volunteer and a vet can all say something about the same cat, and they are not making the same kind of claim. “I think I saw a clipped ear” and “I sterilised this cat on 14 May” differ in kind. If the app simply let each new contribution overwrite the last, a vet's record could be replaced by a guess, and your figures would be worth nothing to a funder.

So the app keeps every observation rather than replacing one with another. Each is dated, attributed, and usually carries the photograph that prompted it. The cat's status is then worked out from all of them together, and the app always shows how confident it is:

LevelMeansExample
ConfirmedSomeone authoritative said soA vet recorded the operation
LikelySeveral people independently agreeThree spotters have seen a clipped ear
ReportedOne person, unconfirmedA spotter thinks she may be pregnant
UnknownNobody has saidMost cats, at the start

The practical effect is that the data improves on its own as the app is used. Every additional observation moves a cat closer to a settled answer, without anybody having to review anything. A cat nobody has looked at closely sits at “unknown”, and a cat several people have independently reported the same thing about becomes “likely” by itself.

Authority is specific rather than general. A vet's word settles whether a cat has been sterilised and carries no special weight on where it sleeps. A guardian's word about their own cat's condition outranks a passing spotter's, and never outranks a vet's on surgery.

Nothing is ever deleted. A correction is added on top and the original stays visible, so the record shows how an answer was reached rather than hiding the disagreement.

One rule worth stating explicitly, because a great deal rests on it:

A clipped ear tip means the cat has almost certainly been sterilised. No visible clip means very little, because plenty of sterilised cats have no clip, or were photographed from the wrong side.

So the app treats a clipped ear as strong evidence on its own, and treats “no clip visible” as no evidence at all rather than as evidence against. Correct us if that does not match what you see in practice.

Contributions

How spotters help without being asked to do work

The app looks at each photograph and can often tell what colour a cat is, what pattern it has, and whether an ear looks clipped. So it proposes, and the person confirms:

One quick thing: is Agatha's ear clipped?

Yes, clipped  ·  No, looks whole  ·  Can't tell

One tap. “Can't tell” is a perfectly good answer and stops the app asking forever. It appears after they've earned their points, never before, and only ever one question at a time, never a form.

The same approach covers appearance. A spotter confirms the cat's colour, its pattern and its distinguishing marks the same way, usually just by agreeing with what the app has already worked out. Those details matter more than they look: they are what lets the app tell two similar cats apart, and what lets you search for “the tortoiseshell near the bakery”.

One safeguard worth knowing about. When the app suggests an answer and someone agrees with it, we record that the suggestion came first. Five people agreeing with the same suggestion is weaker evidence than five people noticing the same thing independently, and your sterilisation figure should not treat the two as equal. For obvious things like coat colour the app simply records its own reading, because it is more reliable than a guess from someone who has never seen a tortoiseshell before.

Welfare

Reporting a cat in trouble

Separate from the game, always available, never buried: this cat needs help. A photograph, the location, and a choice between needs attention and urgent.

Where these usually fail is the middle step, so urgent reports notify volunteers straight away rather than sitting in a list, and others go to whoever is nearest or covers that area. A volunteer marks it seen, acting or resolved, and the person who reported it is told what happened. Without that last step people stop reporting, and it costs almost nothing to include.

We need your guidance on two things: what should happen to an urgent report at two in the morning, and whether you want spotters to see that a report exists at all, or only the person who made it.

Places

Colonies and the feeding network

Colonies. The app groups cats by where they're actually seen, and you name the groups. That way the names match the colonies you already talk about, and nothing has to be redrawn every time a cat moves. All the reporting groups by colony, because that's the unit you plan in.

Feeding sites. Where cats are fed, whether fixed stations or informal spots, which colony each serves, and what condition it's in. Cats gather predictably where they are fed, so this is also a map of where you can reliably find the cats you need to trap.

Feeders. The people who provision those sites. You can record their name and contact details with their consent, recorded properly and withdrawable at any time.

One thing we'd advise on, and want your view. We think feeding sites and feeders should be visible to you and your volunteers, and not to the public. Two reasons. People who feed street cats sometimes face hostility from neighbours, and publishing their locations hands that over. And known feeding sites attract abandonment, since people leave unwanted cats and kittens where they know cats get fed, which makes your problem worse in exactly the places you're working hardest. You'll know better than us how real those risks are on Syros.

Your team

What the app can do for your team

You'll go on running most of your work the way you run it now, and the app doesn't ask you to change that. What it can do is make some of it easier, using information it already holds, so nothing here asks anyone to type something twice. Take up as much or as little of it as suits you, whenever it suits you.

The data settles itself. You do not have to review or approve what spotters report. As more people observe the same cat, the app becomes more certain on its own, and your volunteers only get involved where it matters: correcting something that looks wrong, or recording something only they can know, such as a clinic visit. If nobody from your team ever opened the app, the record would still improve every week.

Trapping rounds. Choose a colony and the app can already tell you which cats aren't sterilised, where they're usually seen, which feeding site they use and when each was last seen. That's most of a target list, assembled without anyone compiling it.

What a cat has cost. Because vet visits are recorded against the cat they were for, the cost per cat adds up on its own. That's the figure funders ask for, and the one that makes “€60 sterilises a cat” a fact rather than an estimate.

Institutional memory. Small organisations lose a great deal when a long-standing volunteer moves away. Anything recorded here survives that, which is rarely the reason anyone adopts something and often the biggest benefit two years later.

Two honest cautions. Every feature here asks somebody to record something, so each one has to save more time than it costs. If it doesn't, don't use it, and tell us. And if any of your work does come to rest on this app, you should be able to get everything out of it in a usable form at any time, and carry on without it. We'd rather write that down now than have you discover it later.

Impact

Reporting what you've achieved

Every figure the app produces carries its own confidence, and says plainly what each part means:

142 cats known on Syros

89 confirmed sterilised, by a vet or by your own records
23 probably sterilised: a clipped ear seen by several people, not yet confirmed
30 with no information either way

Because every observation is dated and attributed, the app can answer “how many cats did we sterilise between January and December” rather than merely asserting it. That's exportable, with the method stated, for funding applications and annual reports.

For your own planning: sterilisation coverage colony by colony, which colonies are growing, which have no feeder, and which parts of Syros nobody has ever photographed. That last is both a gap in your data and something the app can actively send spotters to fill.

For press and supporters: milestones worth telling people about, like the hundredth cat, or a colony reaching full coverage.

Counting every sterilisation on the island, not only yours

We'd like to offer the island's vets their own way in. A vet with an account could record every cat they sterilise, with its home location, whether or not the cat came through Syros Cats.

That turns your reporting from “cats we have sterilised” into “cats on Syros that are sterilised”, which is a far stronger thing to put in front of a funder or a municipality, because it describes the actual state of the island rather than the scale of one organisation's activity. It also stops your volunteers being sent to trap cats that a private owner had done last year. The cost to a vet is a few seconds per operation, and the resulting picture is one nobody currently has.

Engagement

Points, badges and streaks, and why an app like this needs them

This section assumes no familiarity with these ideas. They are the standard machinery of apps that people use voluntarily, and they are the reason a visitor photographs the fourth cat rather than stopping at the first.

The problem they solve is simple. Nobody is paid to use this app, and nothing obliges anyone to keep going. Left alone, most people would photograph one cat, enjoy it, and never open the app again. Everything below exists to convert a single pleasant moment into a habit that lasts a week, or a season.

IdeaWhat it isWhat it does for you
PointsA number that goes up each time someone does something useful, such as recognising a known cat or finding a new one.Tells people, immediately, that what they just did mattered. Also lets us steer effort: things you need more of can be worth more.
BadgesSmall awards for reaching a milestone, such as meeting ten cats or being the first to find one in a neighbourhood.Gives people goals beyond the next photograph, and a reason to go somewhere they otherwise wouldn't.
StreaksA count of consecutive days on which someone has spotted at least one cat.The single strongest reason people open an app again the next day. Nobody likes to break a run.
LeaderboardA ranking of spotters, over a week, a month or a season.Competition, and recognition for the people contributing most.
Levels or standingA status that rises as someone's observations prove reliable.Rewards accuracy rather than volume, and identifies your best future volunteers.

The important design principle, and the reason this section matters to you: whatever earns points is what people will go and do. So the points must be pointed at what you actually need. Recognising a cat that is already in the catalogue has to be worth nearly as much as naming a new one, or people will keep declaring cats “new”, and every duplicate makes your island count, your sterilisation percentage and your trapping list wrong. Cats nobody has checked on for months, health details nobody has confirmed and neighbourhoods nobody has surveyed can all be made worth more, with the reason shown. Used well, the scoring system quietly becomes your task list.

Reach

Getting the island mapped, and keeping people coming back

The problem to solve

Collecting cats is a game that ends. Once someone has met the cats near them, the app has nothing left to ask: a few days for a visitor, sooner and permanently for a resident. So the app needs reasons to open it that aren't “find a new cat”.

Happily, the best of those reasons is something you need anyway.

Checking on cats nobody has seen lately. A cat unseen for two months is a question; six months is a welfare concern. So the app asks: “Nobody has seen Kyra since April. She's usually near the fish market.” That never runs out, it's genuinely useful to you, and it turns the app from a collection someone finishes into a patch they look after. If we get one thing right for engagement, it's this.

Telling people what happened to their cats. “Agatha, the cat you named in June, was sterilised last week.” This is the only thing that works after someone has gone home, and it is the whole post-trip relationship.

Going where nobody has been. For anyone staying long enough to be systematic, the coverage map turns a walk into a contribution.

Who actually maps the island

Everyone does, and the app depends on that. Residents know their own streets better than any visitor ever will and see the same cats across years. Visitors arrive in large numbers and cover the harbour and the town thoroughly. Neither group, on its own, reaches the parts of Syros that nobody walks through on a short holiday.

That is where longer-staying families, such as those with Boundless Life, are likely to matter most. A family here for one to three months, with children who have structured time and no fixed itinerary, is far more likely to range across Ano Syros, Vari, Galissas and Kini than someone here for four days near the port. They are not a substitute for residents or visitors; they are the channel most likely to fill the gaps the other two leave. And because those cohorts arrive continuously, each intake is a fresh wave of contributors rather than a one-off push.

Things we'd propose doing

  • A coverage map, showing where nobody has looked, so a walk becomes a contribution.
  • QR codes in cafés, hotels and on ferries, so the app is discoverable at the moment someone is looking at a cat. Cheap, and probably the highest-yield item on this list.
  • Cat-friendly business partners, meaning venues that display the app and appear in it.
  • Telling visitors what happened next, long after they've gone home.
  • Schools and organised groups, which section 14 covers properly.

Teaching

What the app teaches, and the citizen science it makes possible

The app has thousands of people's attention for a week each, at the exact moment they're looking at a street cat. That's a rare opportunity to change what people understand and do, and we'd rather use it than fill it with trivia.

Everything below is a concrete proposal. Please cut, correct and add: the substance is your expertise, not ours.

Real science, done by children

This is the idea we'd most like to build the educational side around, because it is the one that makes the app worth a school's or a family's time rather than merely enjoyable.

A child using this app is not pretending to be a scientist. They are doing the actual work: observing an animal carefully, recording what they see, distinguishing one individual from another, noticing that their evidence can be wrong, and contributing to a dataset that a real organisation uses to make real decisions. That is what field biology is, and almost no child ever gets to do it.

What that gives a programme like Boundless Life: a genuine field study running for the length of a stay, with a defined territory, a real research question, data the children collect themselves, and a visible result at the end. Not a project about cats. A contribution to an island's animal welfare programme that would not have happened without them, and that they can point at afterwards.

It also teaches the harder lesson underneath: that evidence has different strengths, that one person's sighting is weaker than three, and that “I'm not sure” is a legitimate and valuable answer. The app is built on exactly that principle, so children learn it by using the thing rather than being told.

Practically: a cohort can be given a neighbourhood to survey, a target number of cats, and their own view of what they have contributed. At the end they get a summary of what their group found, how much of the island's record they are responsible for, and what Syros Cats did with it.

The feeding nudge

The most valuable thing the app can say, at the only moment it will land.

Where it appears: in the spot flow, on the screen where someone has just photographed a cat and is waiting a few seconds for the app to identify it. Not as a pop-up, not on every spot, and never before they've had their result. It also appears on a cat's own page, quietly, under the cat's details, and once during the app's introduction.

Thinking of sharing your lunch?

We'd gently ask you not to. Here's why, and it takes thirty seconds.

And then, briefly and without lecturing:

  • The cats are fed already. Syros Cats and local guardians feed these colonies properly, with the right food, in the right places, all year round.
  • Summer feeding creates a winter problem. This is the part visitors never see. A colony fed generously by tourists through July and August grows accustomed to an abundant, easy food source and comes to rely on people for it. In October that food source gets on a ferry and goes home. The cats do not, and the island cannot support the numbers that the summer encouraged. The animals that suffer for it do so in the months when nobody is watching.
  • The wrong food makes them ill. Bread, milk, cured meat and fish bones all do harm.
  • Scattered food draws cats to roads and creates friction with neighbours, who then want the colony removed.

Critically, it ends with something to do instead. “Syros Cats feeds this colony properly, every day, including in winter. €1 covers a cat for a day.” A nudge that only says “don't” is a nudge people ignore. One that redirects the impulse gives the person a way to act on exactly the feeling that made them reach for the sandwich.

We think this may be the single highest-impact feature in the whole app, and it costs almost nothing to build.

Facts, in the seconds people are already waiting

While the app works out which cat it's looking at, there's a short wait. Rather than a spinner, a card, and where possible one about the cat just photographed, because that's the version people remember:

  • Almost every tortoiseshell is female, because the gene for orange sits on the X chromosome.
  • That notch in her ear isn't an injury. It's how vets mark a cat that's been sterilised, done under anaesthetic so she never feels it.
  • White cats with blue eyes are often deaf, which is why this one didn't run when you approached.
  • Cats have a scent map of the island we can't see. He knows exactly where he is.
  • A cat's “hello” is the upright tail. The slow blink means she trusts you.

Strand 3 in full: why sterilisation works

Most people arrive with one of two instincts: that something should be done about the number of cats, or that nothing should be done to them at all. Both are addressed by the same explanation, and it is worth giving properly.

  • The arithmetic. A single unsterilised female and her surviving descendants can become dozens of cats within a few years, because cats can have two or three litters a year and their daughters begin breeding within months. Shown as a simple growing figure rather than a claim, this does the persuading on its own. It also explains why the situation on the island is not anybody's fault: it is what cats do, unless somebody intervenes.
  • Why removing cats fails. If cats are taken away from a place with food and shelter, other cats move in from surrounding areas and breed up to fill it, so the numbers return within a season. This is well established and it is the single most common misunderstanding, including among people who mean well. Sterilising the colony instead leaves the same cats in place, holding their territory, no longer breeding, and gradually declining as they age.
  • What actually happens to the cat. It is trapped humanely, sterilised under anaesthetic by a vet, has the tip of one ear neatly removed while it is asleep so that everyone can see at a glance it has been done, is allowed to recover, and is returned to the exact place it came from. That last part matters: the cat goes home, to the territory and the colony it knows.
  • Why the ear tip is a good thing to see. It means the cat is not going to have kittens, will not fight over mating, will roam less and live longer. A clipped ear is a cat that somebody has already helped.
  • What it costs and what it buys. The link between one donation of €60 and a cat that will not have four more litters, each of which would have needed the same again.

Delivered as five short pieces rather than an essay, each attached to a moment where it makes sense: the ear-tip explanation when someone photographs a clipped ear, the arithmetic when they meet a kitten.

Cat jokes

Sparingly, in the places where nothing else is happening, such as an empty screen or a loading moment. Enough to give the app a personality, rare enough not to wear out. One constraint worth flagging: jokes don't translate. A pun that works in English is not a pun in Greek or French, so each language needs its own, written by someone who speaks it. We'd welcome yours.

Giving

Donations

When you ask matters more than how. The strongest moment by a distance is after someone has gone home and learns their cat was helped, because the outcome comes first and the ask follows it. Second is the end of a trip, alongside “the 14 cats you met on Syros”. A donate button on a help screen, asking in the abstract, is the weakest of the options.

The ladder matters more than any single rung. A €60 ask converts a few people and loses the rest in silence; a €1 ask converts almost anyone and begins a relationship. The first donation's job is to make the second one possible.

We'd keep the petrol tier distinct, because it funds your volunteers rather than the cats: the unglamorous cost nobody normally offers to cover, and one some donors specifically prefer.

One practical note on the €1 tier. Card processing takes a fixed fee per payment, so a single €1 donation loses a large share of itself. The fix isn't to drop it, since it converts best, but to sell the outcome and bill it sensibly: “feed a cat for a month, €30” is one payment, keeps the €1-a-day framing, and gives you regular income instead of a one-off.

Sponsoring a cat in extended care

We had considered letting someone sponsor any individual street cat, and we'd advise against it. A sponsor paying monthly for a cat that is spotted twice a year, with nothing new to report in between, will conclude they have been taken for granted, and they would be right.

What does work is sponsoring the cats that are genuinely with you, in extended care. A cat convalescing at Syros Cats for months after an amputation has a real story, a real and continuing cost, and something new to say every week. That is a sponsorship worth having on both sides.

How it would work. A small number of featured cats in long-term care, each with its own page: what happened, what treatment it is having, what the care costs, and updates as it recovers. People can contribute to that cat's care, or fund a specific operation. When the cat recovers, everyone who helped is told, and they see the outcome they paid for.

This is the strongest fundraising product in the app, and it is honest, because the cost is genuinely ongoing and there is genuinely something to report.

Other ways to give

  • Sponsor a sterilisation, which is specific and completable: someone pays for a named cat's operation and is later told it happened. Because €50 to €60 is a decision rather than an impulse, letting several people co-fund one cat matters. “Agatha's sterilisation: €38 of €60, four supporters” lets someone give €10 and still be part of a specific outcome.
  • Give it as a gift, such as a sterilisation in someone's name, which works for Christmas and for “in memory of”.
  • Sharing and volunteering, for the people who have time rather than money. Adoption exists too, though realistically it will be rare enough that we would not build the app around it.
  • Show where it went, tying every euro back to cats in the record.

Donations earn points, weighted sensibly. Giving is a genuine contribution and the app should recognise it. The weighting has to be chosen with some care, so that money supplements effort rather than replacing it, and nobody can simply buy the top of the leaderboard. We'd suggest donation points count toward badges and recognition but sit on their own footing in the rankings, and we'd want your view.

Two practical constraints. Apple and Google take a substantial cut of anything bought inside an app, and getting charitable donations treated correctly requires following their rules precisely, which usually means donations happen outside the app itself. Done carelessly this gets the app rejected from the stores. And you'd need to tell us how donations reach you, and what receipts or acknowledgements Greek charity rules require.

Privacy

Looking after people's data

The app is designed to know as little about people as it can. Spotters are anonymous, with no name, no email and no account, and that is deliberate.

This is a design decision with a purpose. The less personal data the app collects, the fewer GDPR obligations attach to it, and the less there is for anyone to lose. We have kept collection to the minimum the app genuinely needs. Where personal data is unavoidable, the app is designed to help you comply: a stated purpose for each item, real deletion when someone asks, retention limits, and access confined to your team.

What would be stored about people: guardians' addresses, and optionally their names and contact details. Feeders' names and contact details, with consent. Volunteer and vet accounts.

Who sees it: you and your team. None of it appears anywhere in the public app, which shows only chosen display names.

Where cats live

The app shows where each cat is usually seen. For most cats that is harmless and useful, but poisoning incidents are not unknown in Greece, and wildlife apps routinely blur locations for animals that could be harmed by people knowing where they are.

So we'd propose showing an approximate location publicly for any cat you flag as at risk, while the exact location stays visible to Syros Cats, your volunteers, the cat's guardian, and any signed-in user you choose to trust. The blurring applies only to the anonymous public view, so it never gets in the way of the people who need to find the animal.

Your call on two things: whether you want this at all, and which cats or areas it should apply to. Also worth your view: photographs taken in public sometimes include people in the background, and children use apps like this. Both are manageable, and both are easier handled by choice than by complaint.

Recognition

Telling one cat from another

The app compares a new photograph against cats it already knows. This matters more than it appears: if the same cat gets registered three times, every number in section 11 is wrong, including the island count, the sterilisation proportion, and the list of cats you think still need trapping.

So recognition gets more attention than a photo-matching feature normally would. The app compares against every cat on the island rather than only nearby ones, so a cat that has moved is still found. Its accuracy is measured against sightings people have already confirmed, since every confirmed sighting is effectively someone saying “yes, these two photographs are the same cat”, which is exactly what is needed to test it.

One honest limitation: a great many Greek street cats are tabbies who genuinely look alike, and some pairs will defeat any system. The app is designed to say “I'm not sure” clearly rather than guess, and to let a person settle it.

Later

Souvenirs, as a possible future opportunity

Not part of the first release, and not part of the version after it. We are recording it here because it could become a useful income stream once the app is established, and because it is worth knowing it is possible while other decisions are being made. Nothing below needs a decision now.

Someone who has spent a week meeting the cats of Syros forms a genuine attachment to specific animals. At the moment they leave, they want something, and there is nothing to give them.

The idea is to let people order a physical keepsake showing a cat they photographed, printed and posted to their home address by a print-on-demand partner: a magnet, a mug, a print, a tote bag, a set of postcards. And a calendar, Twelve Cats of Syros, published each autumn, which is the classic charity fundraiser and would write itself from a year of photographs.

How it would make money. Print-on-demand partners charge a base cost per item and let the seller set the retail price, so the difference is yours. A mug costing around €9 to print and post within the EU can sell for €22 to someone who wants a picture of a cat they met, so a margin in the region of half the retail price is realistic, with calendars better still.

What would need thinking through. Whose photograph it is, where the simplest and fairest answer is that people can only order souvenirs of photographs they took themselves. Whether phone photographs print well enough, which they do for small items and not for large ones. Which partner, where the requirements are EU-based production, automated ordering and help with VAT. And whether you want to be the seller, keeping the margin and the admin, or let a partner run it and share the revenue.

The reason this is a later question: a souvenir shop is a small commercial operation with returns, complaints and postal mishaps attached, and it should never become something that consumes your time. It is worth doing only once the app itself is working and there is somebody to hand the day-to-day handling to.

Boundaries

What we're deliberately not building

So expectations are clear:

  • A system to run your organisation. The app can make parts of your work easier using what it already knows. It is not accounting, payroll, stock control or a task manager, and it doesn't expect you to change how you work.
  • Public sign-up. Spotters stay anonymous, permanently. It's what keeps the app effortless and what keeps your data protection obligations small.
  • Anything that asks a spotter to do admin. Every contribution is one tap, optional, and after they've had their fun.
  • A veterinary record system. Vets record outcomes here; their own systems remain their own.
