Purpose

What the app is for

Syros has a large population of street cats and a small number of people caring for them. The gap between those two facts is information. Nobody knows how many cats there are, which have been sterilised, which are in trouble, or where the next effort would do the most good.

Every summer, thousands of people walk the island with cameras in their pockets, photographing cats for the joy of it. This app turns that scattered affection into the record you are missing, and then turns the affection itself into support for your work.

Here is the whole idea in one story. A visitor photographs a tabby in Ano Syros, learns the island has never met her, and names her Agatha. The sighting is filed with a photograph, a location and a date. Over the summer other people meet Agatha and confirm her, and one of them notices her ear is not clipped. In September your team, planning a trapping round, sees an unsterilised female with a reliable feeding spot. In October the visitor, long since home, receives a message saying Agatha was sterilised last week. Under that message is a button that pays for the next cat.

The people it serves

Visitors experience it as a game. They photograph cats, collect the ones they meet, name the new ones and see how they rank.

Residents experience it as recognition. The cat somebody has fed for six years gets a name, a page and a history, and the person who feeds it is named on that page.

Families staying for a season, such as those who come with Boundless Life, are a distinct group. They stay one to three months rather than four days, they range far beyond the harbour, and their children have structured time. They matter more than their numbers suggest, for reasons section 12 explains.

Syros Cats and its volunteers experience none of the above. For your team this is a working tool: which cats are sterilised, which colonies need attention, which animal was just reported injured, and what you can put in front of a funder at the end of the year. Nobody on your team uses this for entertainment, and it is not designed as though they did.

These needs fit together rather than competing. The public side has to stay effortless, because every record your team relies on comes from somebody enjoying themselves. The more enjoyable the app is, the more data you get, the better your reporting becomes, and the stronger the case for donating. Each part feeds the next.

Two ideas that run through everything

The first is citizen science, which means ordinary people, with no training, collecting real observations that an organisation then uses. Birdwatchers counting garden birds on one weekend a year is the best-known example, and their counts feed genuine population studies. A visitor photographing a cat in an alley is doing the same thing. That is what makes this app worth a school's time, not just a tourist's.

The second is that raising engagement and donation income for Syros Cats is an explicit objective, not a side effect. A tourist who has met and named a cat gives more readily than one who has read a poster, and the app is built to convert affection for a specific animal into support for the organisation caring for all of them.

Naming

What we call it, and what we call the people using it

This is worth settling early, because an app store name is painful to change once people are recommending it to each other. A good name here has to work in Greek, English and French, be findable when somebody types “Syros” or “cats” into an app store, be memorable enough to pass between strangers in a café, and survive the app spreading beyond Syros, which matters because you are already talking to organisations elsewhere.

Purropolis joins “purr” to “-opolis”, the Greek for city, and it has a local accident built in: Syros's capital is Ερμούπολη (Ermoúpoli), Hermes' city, so a cat city named the same way belongs here in a way it would belong nowhere else. Because “purr” means nothing in Greek or French, each language gets the same joke remade rather than translated: Γατούπολη follows the Ερμούπολη pattern exactly, and Ronronopolis comes from the French ronron.

Our recommendation is to use two names together. Both app stores allow a name plus a subtitle, in each language. So the app could be Purropolis, subtitled “Syros Cat Spotters”: the distinctive name is what people repeat, and the plain subtitle is what makes it findable. If the conversations with other organisations are serious, we would instead choose “Cat Spotters” as the name, with the place as the subtitle, so that every island runs the same app with a local face. That is a decision about your ambitions rather than about words, and it is question 14 at the end.

One name to avoid is “Syros Cats”. That is your name, and an app carrying it would imply the app is you. That matters if the app ever gets something wrong, and matters more if it ever operates somewhere you do not.

Spotter or snapper?

You suggested “snapper”, from the colloquial sense of snapping a photograph, and it has real charm. Our recommendation is to keep spotter as the role and use snap freely as the verb, for three reasons. The value somebody adds is not the photograph but recognising which cat it is, and a hundred confirmed identifications are a census where a hundred unidentified photographs are not. “Spotter” carries the sense of careful observation, as in birdwatching, which is exactly the citizen science idea above. And it translates into the observing sense in all three languages, where “snapper” translates only into “photographer”.

In short, snapping is what somebody does and spotting is what they become.

People

Who uses it, and what that actually looks like

Nobody needs an account to start. A spotter opens the app and is contributing within a minute. Accounts exist for two reasons: they unlock extra features for people who want them, and they give weight to the word of people whose word should carry weight. Residents can sign up on their own. Every other role comes only by invitation from you, and there is no way for somebody to sign up and declare themselves a vet. If you remove a role, everything that person recorded stays, correctly attributed to them at the time.

A day in each pair of hands

A visitor finds the app through a QR code in a café, and is spotting within a minute of installing it, with no account and no email address. On one walk they photograph four cats: three are already known, so they confirm each and earn points, and one is new, so they name her and earn more. After each spot the app asks one question, such as whether an ear looked clipped, and they answer with a tap or skip it. A streak keeps them opening the app all week. Weeks after they get home, the app tells them a cat they named has been sterilised, and offers a way to pay for the next one.

A resident signs up, claims the cat that lives behind their building, and records that they feed it. Because they see the same cats every day, collecting runs out quickly, so the app asks them different things: whether the cat nobody has seen since April is still around, and whether the thin-looking tom is unwell. Their word about their own street carries more weight than a passing visitor's.

A family staying for a season signs up once, and the children spot under a shared group. The group has a neighbourhood to survey, a target, and its own running score. Along the way the app teaches them to read a clipped ear and to weigh evidence. At the end of the stay they can see exactly what their group added to the island's record.

A cat guardian accepts your invitation and gets their area in one view: which cats have not been seen lately, which have no sterilisation record, which feeding sites need attention. On their rounds they confirm or correct what spotters have reported, and when somebody reports a cat in trouble nearby, they can be among the people who are told.

A Syros Cats volunteer opens a colony before a trapping round and sees which cats are unsterilised, where each is usually found, and which feeding site they gather at. In the field they record outcomes as they happen, with or without signal. They are never asked to review other people's contributions, because the record settles itself, but they can correct anything that looks wrong.

A vet records each sterilisation and vaccination in a few seconds as they perform it, including for cats that never came through Syros Cats. Their entries are definitive and cannot be overwritten by anybody's guess.

Syros Cats management names colonies, sends updates to a cat's followers, pulls the year's figures for a funding application, and uses the maps of unsurveyed and unsterilised areas to decide where the next campaign goes.

The core

Spotting a cat

Somebody photographs a cat, and the app answers one question: have we met this one before? It shows the closest matches, each with a plain explanation such as “the white bib and the notch in the left ear match”. The person confirms one, or says it is nobody they recognise and names the new cat. Names must be unique across the island, and a button suggests unused ones in the user's language. We call the whole exchange a spot.

Confirming a known cat earns points, and naming a new one earns more. Section 12 explains how points, badges and streaks work and why they matter.

The app only compares against cats seen within about 500 metres of the photograph. That is our estimate of how far a street cat ordinarily ranges, and it does two jobs at once. It makes matching more accurate, because the app never considers cats from across the island that this one could not plausibly be, and it makes matching cheaper, because every comparison costs money, as section 16 shows. If 500 metres is wrong for Syros, tell us. You know how far these cats actually roam, and we are estimating.

It works in places with no or limited mobile coverage, which matters because Syros has plenty of those and that is often where the cats are. Photographs, locations and the map itself all work offline, and anything waiting is submitted automatically when the phone finds a connection, dated to when the photograph was taken rather than when it was uploaded. The app does need a connection at some point, because identifying a cat means comparing the photograph against the record, which happens on our side. In practice somebody can walk all day with no signal and have everything identified when they reach a café.

One honest limitation. A great many Greek street cats are tabbies who genuinely look alike, and some pairs will defeat any system. The app is designed to say “I am not sure” clearly rather than guess, and to let a person settle it. Its accuracy is measured against sightings people have already confirmed, because every confirmed sighting is somebody saying two photographs show the same cat, which is exactly what testing needs.

The record

What the app records about each cat

Everything in this section is our assumption rather than your instruction. We have judged each item against two tests: does it help you run your work, and does it help the public stay engaged or learn something. This is the section where your corrections are worth the most, and question 1 at the end asks for them directly.

Each cat has a page: a photograph, a name, a code such as SYR-0042, where it is usually seen, everybody who has ever spotted it, and its history.

Appearance covers colour, pattern and distinguishing features such as a white bib, a notched ear or a kinked tail, all chosen from lists rather than typed, so that a Greek volunteer, a French tourist and an English one describing the same cat produce the same searchable record.

Health covers whether the cat is sterilised, whether its ear is tipped, its vaccinations, its sex, its approximate age, whether it is pregnant or nursing, its general condition, and whether it has died.

People are recorded as specific relationships, because the relationships genuinely differ.

How the cat lives is one status, because not every cat in the record leads the same life, and the difference changes what the app does. It stops the app sending people to find a cat that is recovering indoors, and it shows you at a glance how much of the island's population has somebody keeping an eye on it, which is a different question from how much of it is sterilised.

Cats that quietly disappear

Most street cats are never confirmed dead; they simply stop being seen. If the app did nothing about that, the catalogue would grow forever and every percentage calculated against it would slowly become meaningless. So after three years with no sighting, a cat moves automatically to “presumed deceased”. It leaves the active count and stops appearing as a cat to find, while staying fully in the record. If anybody photographs it the next day it returns instantly, and its page says “not seen since March 2025” rather than announcing a death, because a cat that moved and a cat that died look identical from here.

Data quality

How observations become facts you can publish

Everybody using the app collects observations, including about health, and we want as many as possible. The design question is how a large number of contributions of very different reliability become one answer worth publishing. “I think I saw a clipped ear” and “I sterilised this cat on 14 May” are different kinds of claim, and if each new contribution simply overwrote the last, a vet's record could be replaced by a guess and your figures would be worth nothing to a funder.

So the app keeps every observation, each dated, attributed and usually carrying its photograph, and works the cat's status out from all of them together, always showing how confident it is.

LevelMeansExample
ConfirmedSomebody authoritative said soA vet recorded the operation
LikelySeveral people independently agreeThree spotters saw a clipped ear
ReportedOne person, unconfirmedA spotter thinks she may be pregnant
UnknownNobody has saidMost cats, at the start

The record improves on its own as the app is used. Every additional observation moves a cat toward a settled answer without anybody reviewing anything. Authority is specific rather than general: a vet's word settles surgery and carries no special weight on where a cat sleeps, and a guardian's word about their area outranks a passing visitor's without ever outranking the vet. Nothing is ever deleted; a correction is added on top and the original stays visible, so the record shows how an answer was reached.

How a spotter contributes without doing work

The app examines each photograph and can often tell the cat's colour, pattern, and whether an ear looks clipped. So it proposes and the person confirms.

One quick thing: is Agatha's ear clipped?

Yes, clipped  ·  No, looks whole  ·  Can't tell

That is one tap, offered only after the person has their result and their points, never as a form, and “can't tell” is a respectable answer that stops the question being asked again. Appearance is confirmed the same way, usually by agreeing with what the app already worked out, and those details are what helps to identify unique cats with more reliability.

Two safeguards sit underneath. When the app suggested an answer and somebody agreed, we record that the suggestion came first, because five people agreeing with one suggestion is weaker evidence than five people noticing something independently, and your sterilisation figure should not treat them as equal. For plainly visible things such as coat colour, the app records its own reading directly, which is more reliable than a guess from somebody who has never seen a tortoiseshell.

One rule deserves stating on its own, because a great deal rests on it.

A clipped ear tip means the cat has almost certainly been sterilised. No visible clip means very little, because plenty of sterilised cats have no clip, or were photographed from the wrong side.

The app therefore treats a clipped ear as strong evidence on its own, and treats “no clip visible” as no evidence at all rather than as evidence against. Please correct us if that does not match what you see in practice.

Safeguards

Keeping the record clean, and keeping it about Syros

An app that lets anybody contribute needs protection against misuse and against honest mistakes, because everything it publishes reflects on your name rather than ours.

Only on Syros. Somebody outside Syros can browse the catalogue but cannot add or change anything, and a cat cannot be placed at a location off the island, even from a phone that is on it. A catalogue containing cats from three countries would make every island statistic meaningless.

Already built. Offensive names are rejected in English, Greek and French, with checks that are deliberately hard to sidestep, so swapping letters for numbers or writing a Greek word in Latin characters does not get through. Photographs with no cat in them go no further: nothing is catalogued, no cat is created, and no points are earned. Damaged files and files that are not really images are refused, and limits on rapid repeated use stop anybody flooding the app automatically.

Worth adding, in our view. A “report an issue” button on every cat and photograph, because no automatic check catches everything and the people who see a problem first will be your users, plus a simple queue for your team showing what was reported, with the power to hide a photograph or rename a cat. One tap is the difference between an embarrassment lasting an hour and lasting a month.

A question for you: who should receive these reports, and how quickly would you want to know?

Welfare

Reporting a cat in trouble

This sits apart from everything else, always available and never buried. Somebody who finds a cat in trouble takes a photograph, confirms the location, and chooses between “needs attention” and “urgent”.

The step where systems like this fail is the middle one, where a report is filed and nothing visibly happens. So a report must reach a person, that person marks it seen, being dealt with, or resolved, and the person who reported it is told what happened. That last step costs almost nothing and is the difference between somebody reporting the next cat and not bothering.

We have deliberately not decided who gets told, because we do not know how you work. The options include everybody on your team, volunteers within a distance, the guardian for that area, or one person on duty who decides. There is also a real question about whether an urgent report should interrupt somebody's evening at all. Please tell us what would actually work, including what happens at two in the morning, and whether other users should see that a report exists or whether it stays private to the reporter and your team.

Places

Colonies and the feeding network

Colonies. The app groups cats by where they are actually seen, and you name the groups, so the names match the colonies you already talk about and nothing needs redrawing when a cat moves. All reporting is grouped by colony, because that is the unit you plan in.

Feeding sites. The places cats are fed, whether fixed stations or informal spots, with the colony each serves and its condition. Cats gather predictably where they are fed, so this doubles as a map of where the cats you need to trap can reliably be found.

Feeders. The people who provision those sites. You can record a feeder's name and contact details with their consent, recorded properly and withdrawable at any time.

A question we cannot answer ourselves: should feeding sites and feeders be visible to the public, or only to your team? Two arguments for privacy have been put to us: feeders sometimes face hostility from neighbours, and known feeding sites can attract abandonment, because people leave unwanted kittens where they know cats are fed. There is an argument the other way too, since a visible feeding map shows the colonies are looked after. You will know how real each risk is on Syros, and we do not.

Your team

What the app does for your team

You will keep running your work the way you run it now, and the app does not ask you to change that. It makes parts of the work easier using information it already holds, nothing asks anybody to type something twice, and you can take up as much or as little as suits you. Because the record settles itself, as section 6 described, your volunteers are never a bottleneck: if nobody from your team opened the app for a month, the record would still improve every week.

Trapping rounds. Choose a colony and the app already knows which cats are unsterilised, where each is usually seen, which feeding site they use, and when each was last seen. That is most of a target list, assembled without anybody compiling it.

What a cat has cost. Vet visits are recorded against the cat they were for, so cost per cat adds up on its own. That is the figure funders ask for, and it turns “€60 sterilises a cat” from an estimate into a fact.

Institutional memory. Small organisations lose a great deal when a long-standing volunteer moves away. Anything recorded here survives that, which is rarely why anybody adopts a system and often its biggest benefit two years later.

Two honest cautions. Every feature here asks somebody to record something, so each must save more time than it costs; if one does not, drop it and tell us. And if your work ever comes to rest on this app, you should be able to get everything out in a usable form at any time and carry on without it. We would rather write that down now than have you discover it later.

Every figure the app produces carries its own confidence and says plainly what each part means.

Because every observation is dated and attributed, the app can answer “how many cats did we sterilise between January and December” rather than asserting a number, and the answer can be exported with the method stated, for funding applications and annual reports. If you have existing sterilisation records, we can load them in so the app starts with your history rather than from zero.

For your own planning, it shows sterilisation coverage colony by colony, which colonies are growing, which have no feeder, and which parts of Syros nobody has photographed. For press and supporters, it flags milestones worth telling people about, such as the hundredth cat or a colony reaching full coverage.

Counting every sterilisation on the island, not only yours

We would like to give the island's vets their own way in, so a vet can record every cat they sterilise, with its home location, whether or not it came through Syros Cats.

That turns your reporting from “cats we have sterilised” into “cats on Syros that are sterilised”, which is a far stronger thing to put in front of a funder or a municipality because it describes the state of the island rather than the activity of one organisation. It also stops your volunteers trapping cats a private owner had done last year. The cost to a vet is a few seconds per operation, and the resulting picture is one nobody currently has.

Engagement

What keeps people coming back

Nobody is paid to use this app and nothing obliges anybody to continue. Left alone, most people would photograph one cat, enjoy it, and never return. Everything in this section exists to turn one pleasant moment into a habit, because the census in section 4 only works if people keep spotting.

Whatever earns points is what people will go and do, so the points must be aimed at what you need. Recognising a known cat has to be worth nearly as much as naming a new one, or people will keep declaring cats new, and every duplicate corrupts your island count, your sterilisation percentage and your trapping list. Cats unchecked for months, health details unconfirmed and neighbourhoods unsurveyed can all be made worth more, with the reason shown. Used well, the scoring system quietly becomes your task list.

Reasons to open the app that are not “find a new cat”

Collecting ends. Once somebody has met the cats near them, which takes a visitor days and a resident less, the app needs something else to ask of them, and the best answer is something you need anyway.

Checking on cats nobody has seen lately. A cat unseen for two months is a question, and at six months it is a welfare concern. So the app asks: “Nobody has seen Kyra since April. She is usually near the fish market.” That request never runs out, it is genuinely useful to you, and it turns the app from a collection somebody finishes into a patch they look after. If we get one thing right for engagement, it should be this.

Telling people what happened to their cats is the only feature that works after somebody leaves the island, and it carries the whole relationship afterwards, including the donation moment in section 14. The coverage map shows where nobody has looked, so a walk becomes a contribution.

Who actually maps the island

Everybody does, and the app depends on that. Residents know their streets better than any visitor and watch the same cats across years. Visitors arrive in volume and cover the harbour and town thoroughly. Longer-staying families, such as the Boundless Life cohorts, are the group most likely to fill the gaps the other two leave, because a family here for months, with children who have structured time and no fixed itinerary, ranges across Ano Syros, Vari, Galissas and Kini in a way nobody does in four days. Each arriving cohort is a fresh wave of contributors.

Teaching

What the app teaches, and the science it lets people do

The app holds thousands of people's attention for a week each, at the exact moment they are looking at a street cat. That is a rare chance to change what people understand and do, and we would rather use it than fill it with trivia. Everything below is a concrete proposal: please cut it, correct it and add to it, because the substance is your expertise rather than ours.

Real science, done by citizen scientists, including children

Somebody using this app is not pretending to do science. They are observing an animal carefully, recording what they see, telling one individual from another, discovering that their own evidence can be wrong, and contributing to a dataset a real organisation uses for real decisions. That is field biology, and very few people ever get to do it.

For a programme such as Boundless Life, that is a genuine field study running the length of a stay: a defined territory, a real question, data the users collect themselves, and a visible result. It is not a project about cats; it is a contribution to an island's welfare programme that would not have happened without them. It also teaches the harder lesson underneath, that evidence has different strengths and that “I am not sure” is a valuable answer, and users learn that by using the app rather than by being told.

How the app makes that work. A group is created with a joining code, and anybody who enters it has their spotting counted toward the group as well as themselves. Because every sighting already carries a location, a date and a spotter, the app can display the group's own figures without anybody compiling anything: how many cats found, how many previously unknown, how much of the assigned area covered, and what share of the island's record the group is responsible for. At the end of a stay that becomes a single summary page the group keeps, and you can add a note saying what was done with their work, such as which colonies were visited or how many of their cats have since been sterilised.

Teaching arrives as moments, not lessons

None of this is a course somebody must sit through, because almost nobody would. Each piece is attached to the moment it makes sense: the ear-tip explanation when somebody photographs a clipped ear, the colony arithmetic when they meet a kitten, the approach advice the first time they get close. While the app is identifying a cat there are a few seconds of waiting, and instead of a loading indicator the screen shows one short fact, where possible about the cat just photographed, because that is the version people remember. A teacher who wants everything in one place can read it from a single page.

  • Almost every tortoiseshell is female, because the gene for orange sits on the X chromosome.
  • That notch in her ear is not an injury. It is how vets mark a sterilised cat, done under anaesthetic so she never feels it.
  • White cats with blue eyes are often deaf, which is why this one did not run when you approached.
  • Cats hold a scent map of the island we cannot see. He knows exactly where he is.
  • A cat's greeting is the upright tail. The slow blink means she trusts you.

Strand 3 in full: why sterilisation works

Most people arrive with one of two instincts, either that something must be done about the number of cats or that nothing should be done to them at all. The same explanation answers both.

  • The arithmetic. One unsterilised female and her surviving descendants can become dozens of cats within a few years, because cats can have two or three litters a year and their daughters begin breeding within months. Shown as a growing figure, this persuades on its own, and it makes clear the situation is nobody's fault: it is simply what cats do unless somebody intervenes.
  • Why removing cats fails. Take cats away from a place with food and shelter, and others move in from around it and breed back to the same numbers within a season. This is well established and is the most common misunderstanding, including among people who mean well. Sterilising the colony instead leaves the same cats holding their territory, no longer breeding, gradually declining as they age.
  • What actually happens to the cat. It is trapped humanely, sterilised under anaesthetic by a vet, has the tip of one ear neatly removed while asleep so everybody can see at a glance it has been done, recovers, and is returned to the exact spot it came from. That last part matters: the cat goes home, to the territory and colony it knows.
  • Why an ear tip is good news. It means no kittens, less fighting, less roaming and a longer life. A clipped ear is a cat somebody has already helped.
  • What it costs and what it buys. One donation of €60 becomes a cat that will not have four more litters, each of which would have needed the same again.

The feeding nudge

This may be the single highest-impact feature in the app, and it costs almost nothing to build. It appears in the spotting flow, on the screen where somebody has just photographed a cat and is waiting for the result, never as a pop-up and never on every spot. It also sits quietly on each cat's page and appears once in the app's introduction.

Thinking of sharing your lunch?

We would gently ask you not to. Here is why, and it takes thirty seconds.

The explanation is brief and does not lecture.

  • Feeding by visitors creates a problem that arrives in winter. A colony fed generously by tourists through July and August comes to rely on an easy, abundant food source. In October that food source gets on a ferry and goes home. The cats do not, the island cannot support the numbers a generous summer encouraged, and the animals suffer in the months when nobody is watching.
  • Where colonies are fed properly, it is done consistently. Syros Cats and local feeders provide for a number of colonies with the right food, in the right places, all year round. Where a colony has no feeder, an unpredictable summer supply makes things worse for the same reason.
  • The wrong food makes cats ill. Bread, milk, cured meat and fish bones all do harm.
  • Scattered food draws cats toward roads and creates friction with neighbours, who then want the colony removed altogether.

Critically, it ends with something to do instead. The message closes with a line such as “Syros Cats feeds colonies properly, all year round. €1 covers a cat for a day.” A nudge that only says no is a nudge people ignore. One that redirects the impulse lets the person act on exactly the feeling that made them reach for the sandwich.

Cat jokes

Giving

Donations

When the app asks matters more than how. The strongest moment by far is after somebody has gone home and learns their cat was helped, because the outcome comes first and the request follows it. The second is the end of a trip, beside a summary of the cats they met. A donate button on a help screen, asking in the abstract, is the weakest option, and it is the only one most charity apps have.

Sponsoring a cat in extended care

We considered letting somebody sponsor any street cat, and we advise against it, because a sponsor paying monthly for a cat spotted twice a year will conclude they have been taken for granted, and they would be right. What works is sponsoring the cats genuinely with you: a cat convalescing at Syros Cats for months after losing a leg has a real story, a real continuing cost, and something new to say every week.

How it would work: a small number of featured cats in long-term care, each with a page describing what happened, the treatment, the cost and the recovery. People contribute to that cat's care or fund a specific operation, and when the cat recovers, everybody who helped is told and sees the outcome they paid for. This is the strongest fundraising product in the app, and it is honest, because the cost is genuinely ongoing and there is genuinely something to report.

The rest of it

  • Co-funded sterilisations. Since €50 to €60 is a decision rather than an impulse, a page reading “Agatha's sterilisation: €38 of €60, four supporters” lets somebody give €10 and still buy a specific outcome, and they are told when it happens.
  • Gifts, such as a sterilisation in somebody's name, for Christmas or in memory of someone.
  • Time instead of money: sharing and volunteering, for the people who have one and not the other. Adoption is possible too, though rare enough that it should not shape the design.
  • Showing where it went, with every euro tied back to cats in the record.

Donations earn points, weighted sensibly. Giving is a genuine contribution and the app should recognise it, with the weighting chosen so money supplements effort rather than replacing it and nobody simply buys the top of the leaderboard. We would suggest donation points count toward badges and recognition while sitting on their own footing in the rankings, and we would like your view.

Two practical constraints. Apple and Google take a substantial share of anything bought inside an app, and having charitable donations treated correctly means following their rules precisely, which usually means the donation itself happens outside the app. Done carelessly, this gets an app rejected from the stores. Separately, you would need to tell us how donations reach you and what receipts Greek charity rules require.

Privacy

Looking after people's data

The app is designed to know as little about people as it can. Spotters are anonymous, with no name, email address or account, and that is a deliberate choice: the less personal data the app holds, the fewer data protection obligations attach to it and the less there is for anybody to lose. Where personal data is genuinely necessary, the app is built to help meet those obligations, with a stated purpose for each item, real deletion on request, retention limits, and access confined to the people who need it.

The personal data held is limited to the addresses of cat guardians and optionally their names and contact details, the names and contact details of feeders with their consent, and accounts for volunteers, vets and your team. None of it appears in the public app, which shows only chosen display names.

Who is responsible under GDPR

Because Fair Shake would provide and operate the app for you as a managed service, responsibility divides. Data protection law distinguishes the organisation that decides why data is collected, called the controller, from the organisation that handles it on the first one's instructions, called the processor. The controller carries most of the obligations, and the processor carries a narrower set of its own.

Most likely position
Syros CatsController for the data collected for your work: guardians, feeders and volunteers. You decide why those records exist.
Fair ShakeProcessor, operating the app on your instructions, and controller only for the little data that exists to run the service itself.

In practice that means a short written agreement between us covering security, help with deletion requests, breach notification and the suppliers behind the service, with the data stored in the European Union, which the suppliers we propose all support.

We are not lawyers, and this is our understanding rather than legal advice. The division above is the usual one, but Fair Shake makes many of the design decisions about what gets collected, which can affect the analysis, so it is worth confirming. The agreement is far easier to put in place before launch than after.

Where cats live

The app shows where each cat is usually seen. For most cats that is harmless, but poisoning incidents are not unknown in Greece, and wildlife apps routinely blur the locations of animals that could be harmed by publicity. We would therefore show only an approximate location to the anonymous public for any cat you flag as at risk, while the exact location stays visible to your team, your volunteers, the cat's guardian and feeders, and any signed-in user you choose to trust, so the blurring never hinders the people who might need to find the animal.

Your call on two things: whether you want this, and for which cats or areas. It is also worth your view that photographs taken in public sometimes include people in the background, and that children use apps like this. Both are manageable, and both are easier handled by choice than by complaint.

Later

Souvenirs, as a possible future opportunity

This is not part of the first release or the one after it. We record it because it could become a useful income stream once the app is established, and nothing here needs a decision now.

Somebody who has spent a week meeting the cats of Syros forms a genuine attachment to specific animals, and at the moment they leave there is nothing to give them. The idea is a physical keepsake showing a cat they photographed, printed and posted home by a printing partner: a magnet, a mug, a print, a set of postcards, or an annual “Twelve Cats of Syros” calendar that assembles itself from the year's photographs, which is the classic charity fundraiser.

Printing partners charge a base cost and let the seller set the price, so the difference is yours. A mug costing about €9 to print and post within the European Union sells for €22 to somebody who wants a cat they met, so a margin around half the price is realistic, with calendars better still. The questions to settle later are whose photograph it is, where the fair answer is that people order only their own, which products print well from phone photographs, which partner to use, and whether you sell directly or share revenue with a partner who handles the administration. A souvenir shop brings returns, complaints and postal mishaps with it, and it should never consume your time, which is exactly why it waits.

Boundaries

What we are deliberately not building

These are stated so that expectations are clear.

  • A system to run your organisation. The app makes parts of your work easier using what it already knows. It is not accounting, payroll, stock control or a task manager, and it does not expect you to change how you work.
  • Public sign-up as a requirement. Spotters can always stay anonymous, which keeps the app effortless and the data protection obligations small.
  • Anything that asks a spotter to do administration. Every contribution is one tap, optional, and offered after they have had their fun.
  • A veterinary record system. Vets record outcomes here, and their own systems remain their own.
