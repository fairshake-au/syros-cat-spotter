   1  Syros has a large population of street cats and a small number of people working to care for them. The gap between those two facts is information: which cats exist, where they live, which have been sterilised, which need help, and where the next effort would do the most good.

   2  There are thousands of people on the island every summer who like cats, carry a camera, and walk everywhere. The app turns that attention into a record.

   3  For a spotter , it's a game: photograph a cat, find out whether it's one the island already knows, name it if it's new, collect the ones you've met, and see how you rank.

   4  For a resident , it's recognition: the cat you've fed for six years gets a name, a page and a history, and you're on it.

   5  For you , it's a census that maintains itself — cats, locations, photographs, dates, sterilisation status and welfare concerns, gathered by people who are enjoying themselves rather than working for you.

   6  The principle underneath everything: spotting must stay effortless, or there is no data. Every operational feature in here is built so that it never gets between someone and their fun.

   7  Nobody needs an account to use the app. A spotter opens it and starts. Accounts exist only for people whose word needs to carry extra weight.

   8  Everyone is a spotter — that's the base role, and every other role is added on top of it. A vet is a spotter who can also record surgery. We've deliberately not called it “visitor”, because a good many of the best spotters live here.

  10  Invitations come from you. There is no way to sign up and declare yourself a vet. You send an invitation, the person accepts, and they hold that role until you remove it. If you remove it, everything they recorded stays — correctly attributed to them, at the time they recorded it.

  11  Signing in doesn't put someone into a different app. It adds controls to the app they already have, so a volunteer standing in front of a cat can record what they see without going back to a desk.

  12  Someone photographs a cat. The app compares that photograph against the cats it already knows and asks: have we met this one before?

  13  It shows the closest few matches with a plain-English reason for each — “the white bib and the notch in the left ear match” — and the person confirms one, or says it's nobody they recognise and gives the new cat a name. New names must be unique across the island, and a button suggests unused ones in whichever language they're using.

  14  Confirming a known cat earns points. Naming a new one earns more. Points build streaks, badges and a leaderboard, and that machinery is doing real work: it's what makes someone photograph the fourth cat rather than stopping at the first.

  15  It all works with no signal. Syros has plenty of places with no coverage, and that's exactly where the cats are. Photographs, locations and the map itself work offline; anything waiting is submitted automatically when the phone finds a connection — and the sighting is dated to when the photograph was taken, not when it eventually uploaded.

  16  Each cat has a page: photograph, name, a code like SYR-0042, where it's usually seen, everyone who's ever spotted it, and its history.

  17  Appearance — colour, pattern, and distinguishing features like a white bib, a notched ear or a kinked tail. Chosen from lists rather than typed, so a Greek volunteer, a French tourist and an English one describing the same cat produce the same searchable record rather than three different words. It's also how the app can support the way people actually talk: “the tortoiseshell by the bakery” rather than “SYR-0042” .

  18  Health and care — sterilised, ear-tipped, vaccinated, sex, age, pregnant or nursing, general condition, and sadly sometimes deceased.

  19  Who looks after it — its guardian, if it has one, and where it lives.

  20  How the cat lives. A simple status, because not every cat in the record is living the same kind of life:

  21  Status Means Street Free-roaming, no regular carer Fed Free-roaming, but fed regularly by a guardian or at a feeding station — no owner In care Temporarily off the street — recovering from surgery, being treated, or with a carer Deceased

  22  This matters for more than tidiness. It stops the app inviting people to go and find a cat that's recovering indoors, and it lets you see at a glance how much of the island's population has someone keeping an eye on it — a different question from how much has been sterilised, and just as useful.

  23  We've kept all of this deliberately short. If knowing something wouldn't change what anyone does, it isn't worth asking anyone to record it. If we've left out something you track, that's the single most useful correction you can give us.

  24  This is the part we'd most like you to understand, because it's what makes the numbers defensible.

  25  A tourist, a resident, a volunteer and a vet can all say something about the same cat, and they are not making the same kind of claim. “I think I saw a clipped ear” and “I sterilised this cat on 14 May” differ in kind, and if they overwrite each other in the same box, your figures become unciteable.

  26  So nobody edits a cat's health status. People add observations — dated, attributed, often with the photograph that prompted them. The app works out the status from all of them, and always shows how sure it is:

  27  Level Means Example Confirmed Someone authoritative said so Your vet recorded the operation Likely Several people independently agree Three spotters have seen a clipped ear Reported One person, unconfirmed A spotter thinks she may be pregnant Unknown Nobody has said Most cats, at the start

  28  Authority is specific rather than general. A vet's word settles whether a cat has been sterilised and carries no special weight on where it sleeps. A guardian's word about their own cat's condition outranks a passing spotter's, and never outranks a vet's on surgery.

  29  Nothing is ever deleted. A correction is added on top and the original stays visible. That's deliberate, for a reason you'll recognise: a cat with no ear-tip may well have been sterilised anyway. The record needs to show that being worked out, not quietly hide the disagreement.

  30  Which leads to the rule we'd most like you to check:

  31  An ear-tip that's visible is strong evidence a cat was sterilised. An ear-tip that isn't visible is evidence of almost nothing at all.

  32  The app will never move a cat toward “not sterilised” because people can't see a clip — no matter how many people can't see it. Only you or a vet can say a cat hasn't been done. Getting this the wrong way round would put every unclipped-but-sterilised cat on your trapping list and send volunteers out for nothing, which is the worst thing this app could do to you.

  40  Where these usually fail is the middle step — so urgent reports notify volunteers straight away rather than sitting in a list, and others go to whoever is nearest or covers that area. A volunteer marks it seen, acting or resolved — and the person who reported it is told what happened. Without that last step people stop reporting, and it costs almost nothing to include.

  42  Colonies. The app groups cats by where they're actually seen, and you name the groups. That way the names match the colonies you already talk about, and nothing has to be redrawn every time a cat moves. All the reporting groups by colony, because that's the unit you plan in.

  43  Feeding sites. Where cats are fed — fixed stations or informal spots — which colony each serves, and what condition it's in.

  44  This is more useful than it sounds. Cats gather predictably where they're fed, so a map of feeding sites is a map of where you can reliably find and trap the cats you need to sterilise. A colony with no feeder is a welfare problem; a colony with three uncoordinated feeders is a different one.

  49  The day list. A volunteer opens the app and sees what's worth doing here, today : cats due a check, welfare reports assigned to them, and confirmations that would settle a colony's status. Nobody has to schedule any of it — the app works it out from what it already knows.

  50  Trapping rounds. Choose a colony and the app can already tell you which cats aren't sterilised, where they're usually seen, which feeding site they use and when each was last seen. That's most of a target list, assembled without anyone compiling it.

  51  Getting cats home. A sterilised cat has to go back exactly where it came from, and the app knows precisely where. It also works offline, which a note in a van on a clinic day tends not to — volunteers are outdoors more than visitors are, and often with worse signal.

  52  What a cat has cost. Because vet visits are recorded against the cat they were for, the cost per cat adds up on its own. That's the figure funders ask for, and the one that makes “€60 sterilises a cat” a fact rather than an estimate.

  53  Institutional memory. Small organisations lose a great deal when a long-standing volunteer moves away. Anything recorded here survives that — rarely the reason anyone adopts something, and often the biggest benefit two years later.

  54  What we won't build: accounting, payroll, stock control, or a general task system. You either have ways of doing these or don't need them, and each one would add something for you to maintain in exchange for very little.

  55  And two honest cautions. Every feature here asks somebody to record something, so each one has to save more time than it costs — if it doesn't, don't use it, and tell us. And if any of your work does come to rest on this app, you should be able to get everything out of it in a usable form at any time, and carry on without it. We'd rather write that down now than have you discover it later.

  56  Every figure the app produces carries its own confidence:

  57  142 cats known · 89 confirmed sterilised · 23 likely · 30 unknown

  58  It's tempting to publish the round number. The honest one is stronger — a funder who can see the uncertainty trusts the total, and a spotter who sees 30 unknowns sees something they can personally help with.

  59  Because every observation is dated and attributed, the app can answer “how many cats did we sterilise between January and December” rather than merely asserting it. That's exportable, with the method stated, for funding applications and annual reports.

  60  For your own planning: sterilisation coverage colony by colony, which colonies are growing, which have no feeder, and — free of charge — which parts of Syros nobody has ever photographed. That last is both a gap in your data and something the app can actively send spotters to fill.

  61  For press and supporters: milestones worth telling people about, like the hundredth cat, or a colony reaching full coverage.

  62  The problem we have to solve

  63  Collecting cats is a game that ends. Once someone has met the cats near them, the app has nothing left to ask — a few days for a visitor, sooner and permanently for a resident. So the app needs reasons to open it that aren't “find a new cat”.

  64  Happily, the best of those reasons is something you need anyway.

  65  Checking on cats nobody has seen lately. A cat unseen for two months is a question; six months is a welfare concern. So the app starts asking: “Nobody has seen Kyra since April — she's usually near the fish market.” That never runs out, it's genuinely useful to you, and it changes the app from a collection someone finishes into a patch they look after. If we build one thing for engagement, it's this one.

  66  Telling people what happened to their cats. “Agatha, the cat you named in June, was sterilised last week.” This is the only thing that works after someone has gone home — the whole post-trip relationship, and, not coincidentally, the honest moment to mention donating.

  67  Going where nobody has been. For anyone staying long enough to be systematic, the coverage map turns a walk into a contribution.

  74  The feeding nudge, at the moment it matters

  75  Someone standing in front of a hungry cat holding half a sandwich is the only audience that will ever really take this in, so that is where it's said — briefly, at the point of spotting, with the reasons a tap away:

  76  Human food makes cats ill. Cats can't digest milk, and salty or processed food does real harm. Food left out draws rats and gulls, and causes trouble with neighbours and businesses — which makes life harder for the cats, not easier. Cats crowding at an unmanaged food source pass disease to each other. It pulls cats towards roads and busy tourist areas. You run proper feeding, with the right food in the right places.

  84  Rewarding the right things

  85  Points aren't decoration; they steer behaviour. So they point at what you need.

  86  Recognising a known cat is worth nearly as much as naming a new one. This matters more than it sounds: if discovery paid much better, people would effectively be paid to declare cats unknown — and every duplicate makes your island count, your sterilisation percentage and your trapping list wrong. The gap stays deliberately small.

  87  Points follow need. The app knows which cats haven't been seen in months, which have unknown health, and which areas are unsurveyed — so those are worth more, with the reason shown. In effect the scoring is your task list , and people chasing points do work you'd otherwise be asking volunteers to do.

  88  There's recognition for being accurate rather than merely busy — someone whose reports your volunteers keep confirming visibly earns standing — and time-limited leaderboards, so a visitor arriving in August is playing for something they can actually win.

 102  Souvenirs, printed and posted home

 115  Three practical warnings.

 116  Card processing takes a small percentage and a fixed fee per transaction, so very small donations lose a disproportionate amount — worth setting sensible minimums.

 117  Apple and Google take a substantial cut of anything bought inside an app , and getting charitable donations treated correctly means following their rules precisely — usually with donations happening outside the app itself. Done carelessly, this gets the app rejected from the stores.

 118  And you'd need to tell us how donations reach you, and what receipts Greek charity rules require.

 119  The app compares a new photograph against cats it already knows. This matters more than it appears: if the same cat gets registered three times, every number in section 10 is wrong — the island count, the sterilisation proportion, and the list of cats you think still need trapping.

 120  So it's built to be checkable rather than taken on faith. Every time someone confirms a match, they are effectively saying “yes, these two photographs are the same cat” — which gives a growing body of human-verified answers to test the recognition against, and to keep testing it as the catalogue grows. We will always be able to tell you how accurate it is , rather than assuring you that it works.

 121  The app also gives itself a visual memory of every cat on the island, so a cat that has wandered to the other side of Ermoupoli is still recognised — matching on what a cat looks like rather than only on where it was last seen.

 122  One honest limitation: a great many Greek street cats are tabbies who genuinely look alike, and some pairs will defeat any system. The app is designed to say “I'm not sure” clearly rather than guess, and to let a person settle it.

 123  For ordinary users the app deliberately knows almost nothing about anybody — no names, no emails, no accounts. But the roles in section 2 do involve holding personal details, and that should be decided deliberately rather than by accident.

 124  What would be stored about people: guardians' addresses, and optionally their names and contact details. Feeders' names and contact details, with consent. Volunteer and vet accounts.

 125  Who sees it: you and your team. None of it appears anywhere in the public app, which continues to show only chosen display names.

 131  The findable/memorable tension has a standard resolution: a brand name plus a descriptive subtitle. Both app stores support exactly this — a short name and a separate subtitle line — so we can have “Purropolis” for the memory and “Cats of Syros” for the search, without choosing.

 132  The recommendation: one joke, remade in three languages

 133  Purropolis is purr + -opolis , the Greek for city. It has a lovely local accident built into it: Syros's own capital is Ερμούπολη — Ermoupoli, “Hermes' city”. A cat-city named the same way sits naturally on this island in a way it wouldn't anywhere else.

 134  But the pun only half-survives translation. “Purr” means nothing in Greek — the Greek for purring is γουργουρητό — so a Greek speaker gets the “city” and misses the cat. The same is true in French.

 135  So rather than transliterating one name into three alphabets, we'd remake the same joke in each language , keeping one shape:

 136  Language Name The joke English Purropolis purr + city Greek Γατούπολη γάτα (cat) + -ούπολη — exactly the Ερμούπολη pattern French Ronronopolis ronron (purr) + city

 137  Γατούπολη is the strongest of the three and we'd want your ear on it, because it's the one that has to be right. It's the pun a Greek would actually make, it echoes Ermoupoli directly, and it needs no explanation to anyone on the island.

 138  This costs nothing extra: both app stores let you set a different name per language, and the app already translates everything else. The wordmark is set in type rather than drawn as a logo, so it can be set in Greek script just as easily as Latin.

 141  One thing to avoid: not “Syros Cats”. That's your name, and an app carrying it would imply the app is you — which matters if it ever gets something wrong, and matters more if it ever operates somewhere you don't.
