For media

An island is naming every cat on it

Syros is building a name-by-name register of every street cat on the island — and the people doing the fieldwork are the tourists who were photographing those cats anyway.

For editors and journalists Syros, Cyclades, Greece September 2026

The story

Every place facing cat overpopulation has two challenges: a large population of street cats, and a small number of people looking after them. The gap between them is information, so the work gets planned from memory rather than evidence. Cat Spotters, designed by Fair Shake with the volunteers of Syros Cats, closes that gap from an unexpected direction: it turns the thousands of photographs visitors already take into dated, located, identified records — so Syros Cats can see where the cats actually are, and send volunteers where a morning of their time will do most good.

A visitor photographs a tabby in Ano Syros, learns the island has never met her, and names her Agatha. Others confirm her over the summer, and one notices her ear is not clipped. In September the round above Ano Syros brings up an unsterilised female: it is Agatha. In October the visitor, long since home, is told Agatha was sterilised last week.

How it works

Cat Spotters opens in a browser at catspotters.com and installs from the App Store and Google Play. It is free, runs in English, Greek and French, and needs no account.

Photograph a cat

The app answers one question: have we met this one before? It shows the closest matches with a plain reason such as “the white bib and the notch in the left ear match”, and the person confirms one or names a new cat. Photographs, locations and the map work with no connection, which matters on an island full of places that have none.

A game that keeps people going

Spotters earn points, badges and streaks, the same machinery Duolingo uses. Groups — a school, a family, a visiting cohort — join with a code and take a neighbourhood.

Anyone can report a cat in trouble

A photograph, a location, and a choice between “needs attention” and “urgent”. Wherever it can, the app closes the loop: the report reaches a person, and whoever made it hears back.

Why it matters

It is real citizen scienceOrdinary people with no training collecting observations that inform real decisions — the same idea as the garden bird counts that feed real population studies. Every observation is dated and attributed, so Syros Cats can answer “how many cats were sterilised this year” with the method stated rather than asserting a number.
It teaches at the right momentWhile a photograph is identified, the screen shows a short fact instead of a spinner: what a clipped ear means, why sterilisation is the humane answer, why sharing your lunch in August harms a cat in November.
It converts affection into actionA donation of €50–60 sterilises one cat, and each kitten of the four litters it will now not have would need the same again. The app asks at the two moments feeling runs highest: on the spot, and when somebody hears their cat was helped.

Two things to get rightThe ear tip is not an injury — it is how vets mark a sterilised cat, done under anaesthetic. And the app does not ask visitors to feed cats; it asks them not to, and explains why.
