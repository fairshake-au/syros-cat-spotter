The story

Anywhere with too many street cats has the same two problems: a lot of cats, and very few people looking after them. What sits between them is information. Nobody knows how many cats there are, where they are, or which have been sterilised — so the work gets planned from memory, and from whoever complained most recently.

Cat Spotters closes that gap from an unexpected direction. Every summer, visitors to Syros take thousands of photographs of its cats, and every one of them goes home in a pocket. The app turns those photographs into dated, located, identified records — so Syros Cats can see where the cats actually are, and send a volunteer where a morning of their time does the most good.

A visitor photographs a tabby in Ano Syros, learns the island has never met her, and names her Agatha. Others confirm her over the summer, and one notices her ear is not clipped. How it works

The point of all of it is the last step: Syros Cats uses what the app collects to decide which areas to tackle next, so its volunteers do the most good they can with the time they have.

Photograph a cat

The app answers one question: have we met this one before? It shows the closest matches with a plain reason such as “the white bib and the notch in the left ear match”, and the person confirms one or names a new cat. Photographs, locations and the map work with no connection; identifying the cat needs a signal at some point, which can be later, back at the hotel.

A game that keeps people going

Spotters earn points, badges and streaks, the same machinery Duolingo uses. Groups — a school, a family, a visiting cohort — join with a code and take a neighbourhood.

Where to go next

Every spot is dated and located, so the map fills in: where the cats are, where hardly any have been sterilised, and which streets nobody has walked. That is what turns a morning of volunteer time into the morning that counts.

Why it matters

It is a story about a street, not only about cats

Uncontrolled breeding is hard on the cats first — kittens die of disease, dehydration and cars — then on the people living alongside them, and then on the handful of volunteers trying to keep up with it. Sterilisation is the humane answer and the only one that works; everything else manages how bad it gets.

It is citizen science

Citizen science means ordinary people, with no training, collecting real observations that an organisation then uses. The best-known example is the garden bird count: over one weekend each year, hundreds of thousands of people write down the birds they see from their kitchen windows, and those lists feed genuine research into which species are in decline. A visitor photographing a cat in an alley is doing the same job. Every observation is dated and attributed, so Syros Cats can answer “how many cats were sterilised this year” with the method stated, rather than asserting a number. If the island’s vets opt in, it becomes a count of every sterilised cat on Syros.

It teaches at the moment somebody is standing there

While a photograph is identified, the screen shows a short fact instead of a spinner: what a clipped ear means, why sterilisation is the humane answer, and why sharing your lunch in August harms a cat in November. Then it gives people something to do instead: €50–60 sterilises one cat.

About

Syros Cats

A volunteer organisation caring for the street cats of Syros in the Greek Cyclades. It traps, sterilises and returns cats, treats the sick and injured, and works with the island’s vets and residents. Its work relies on donations and on funding from the Dimos.

Fair Shake

An organisation using technology to make a positive impact. It designed Cat Spotters with Syros Cats and operates it on a pro bono basis, recognising the challenges of cat overpopulation and the untapped potential for the community to help solve it.
