An island is naming every cat on it

Syros is building a name-by-name register of every street cat on the island — and the people doing the fieldwork are the tourists who were photographing those cats anyway.

For editors and journalists Place Syros, Cyclades, Greece September 2026

The story

Every Greek island has the same two facts sitting side by side: a large population of street cats, and a small number of people looking after them. The gap between those facts is information. Nobody knows how many cats there are, which have been sterilised, which are in trouble, or where the next effort would do the most good — so the work is planned from memory and goodwill rather than evidence.

Cat Spotters, built by Fair Shake with the volunteers of Syros Cats, closes that gap from an unexpected direction. Visitors already photograph the island’s cats in their thousands. The app turns each photograph into a dated, located, identified record — and turns the person who took it into a supporter who hears, months later, what happened to the cat they named.

A visitor photographs a tabby in Ano Syros, learns the island has never met her, and names her Agatha. Over the summer other people meet Agatha and confirm her, and one notices her ear is not clipped. In September the Syros Cats team, planning a trapping round, sees an unsterilised female: it is Agatha. In October the visitor, long since home, gets a message saying Agatha was sterilised last week. Under it is a button that pays for the next cat.

How it works

Photograph a cat

The app answers one question: have we met this one before? It compares the photograph against cats known nearby and shows the closest matches, each with a plain reason such as “the white bib and the notch in the left ear match”.

Confirm, or name a new cat

The person either confirms a match or says it is nobody they recognise and names the cat. Names are unique across the island. Confirming earns points; naming a new cat earns more.

Every cat has a page

A photograph, a name, a code such as SYR-0042, where it is usually seen, its health and history, and everybody who has ever spotted it.

A game that keeps people going

Points, badges, streaks and leaderboards. Groups — a school, a family, a visiting cohort — join with a code, take a neighbourhood, and get their own figures and an end-of-stay summary.

Anyone can report a cat in trouble

A photograph, a location and a choice between “needs attention” and “urgent”. The report reaches a person, and the reporter is told what happened — the step most systems like this skip.

No account, three languages

English, Greek and French, with no name or email address required. Photographs, locations and the map work with no signal; queued spots are identified once the phone finds a connection.

Why it matters

It is real citizen scienceOrdinary people with no training collecting observations an organisation genuinely uses — the same idea as the garden bird counts that feed real population studies. Every observation is dated and attributed, and the app always shows how confident it is: confirmed by a vet, likely where several people agree, or reported by one.
It makes sterilisation figures defensibleSyros Cats can answer “how many cats were sterilised this year” with the method stated, rather than asserting a number — the difference between an anecdote and a funding application. Give the island’s vets their own access and it becomes a count of every sterilised cat on Syros, not only the charity’s own.
It teaches at the right momentWhile a photograph is being identified the screen shows one short fact instead of a spinner: what a clipped ear means, why removing cats fails while sterilising a colony works, and why sharing your lunch with a cat in August harms it in November.
It converts affection into moneyA donation of €50–60 sterilises one cat — a cat that will not have four more litters, each of which would have needed the same again. The app asks after the outcome, not before it.
