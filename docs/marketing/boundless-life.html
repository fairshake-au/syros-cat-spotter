A field study on the doorstep, for the length of a stay

A field study that runs the length of a stay, on the doorstep, for children who are already curious about the cats they walk past every day — and a real contribution to the island that would not happen without them.

For Boundless Life — education and community teams Site Syros, Greece September 2026

What the app is

Syros has a large population of street cats and a small number of people caring for them. Nobody knows how many cats there are, which have been sterilised, or where the next effort would do the most good. Cat Spotters closes that gap by turning the photographs people already take into a record the island can use.

You photograph a cat, and the app answers one question: have we met this one before? It shows the closest matches with a plain reason — “the white bib and the notch in the left ear match” — and you either confirm one or name a cat nobody has met before. Confirming earns points, naming a new cat earns more. Every cat gets a page: a photograph, a name, a code such as SYR-0042, where it lives, its health, and everybody who has ever spotted it.

It needs no account and no email address, and it runs in English, Greek and French. Photographs, locations and the map work with no signal, which matters on Syros, and everything waiting is submitted when the phone finds a connection.

A child photographs a tabby above Ano Syros, learns the island has never met her, and names her Agatha. Over the following weeks other people meet Agatha and confirm her, and one notices her ear is not clipped. In September the Syros Cats team, planning a trapping round, sees an unsterilised female: it is Agatha. In October, back home, the family gets a message saying Agatha was sterilised last week.

Why a Boundless Life cohort is a distinctive contributor

Everybody maps the island, and the app depends on that. Residents know their own streets better than any visitor and watch the same cats across years. Tourists arrive in volume and cover the harbour and the town thoroughly. Families staying for a season are a third kind of contributor, and they reach what the other two leave.

A family here for months, with children who have structured time and no fixed itinerary, ranges across Ano Syros, Vari, Galissas and Kini in a way nobody does in four days. Each arriving cohort is a fresh wave of contributors picking up where the last one stopped — and because the record persists, a cohort in April can be told what happened to the cats a cohort in October first found.

What a cohort would actually do

Join with a code

A group is created with a joining code. Anybody who enters it has their spotting counted toward the group as well as themselves. Children can take part with no account of their own; families who want the extras can sign up.

Take a territory

A cohort gets an assigned neighbourhood and a target. A coverage map shows exactly where nobody has looked yet, which turns an ordinary afternoon walk into a contribution and gives a group somewhere specific to go.

Watch the score

The group has its own running figures, updated without anybody compiling anything: cats found, how many previously unknown, how much of the assigned area covered, and what share of the whole island’s record the group is responsible for.

Keep the result

At the end of the stay that becomes a single summary page the group keeps — with a note from Syros Cats saying what was done with the work, such as which colonies were visited and how many of the group’s cats have since been sterilised.

Report what needs help

A cat in trouble can be reported in seconds, and the person who reported it is told what happened. When a kitten appears in a photograph the app opens a litter report and asks the children to count the kittens and look for the mother.

Learn while doing it

Teaching arrives as moments rather than lessons: while a photograph is being identified, the screen shows one short fact instead of a spinner — and where possible it is about the cat just photographed, because that is the version people remember.

The learning underneath

Children using this app are not pretending to do science. They are observing an animal carefully, recording what they see, telling one individual from another, discovering that their own evidence can be wrong, and contributing to a dataset a real organisation uses for real decisions. That is field biology, and very few children ever get to do it. It also teaches the harder lesson: that evidence has different strengths, and that “I’m not sure” is a valuable answer rather than a failure — learnt by using the app, not by being told.

Reading a catColours, patterns and body language: the flat ear, the slow blink, the twitching tail, the upright tail as a greeting, and what each one is saying.
Life in a colonyWhat a colony is, how territory works, who eats first, where cats sleep and why.
Why sterilisation worksThe arithmetic of one unsterilised female; why removing cats fails and sterilising the colony works; what actually happens to a cat, including why the ear tip is good news rather than an injury.
Being around a street catHow to approach one, when not to, why not to pick one up — and why not to share your lunch, which is the hardest one to explain to a child and the one that matters most.
When a cat needs helpWhat the warning signs are, what merely looks alarming, and who to tell.
You are doing scienceWhat happens to the photographs, why some evidence counts for more than other evidence, and what Syros Cats did with it.

The feeding message deserves a note of its own, because it runs against a child’s instinct. A colony fed generously by visitors through July and August comes to rely on it; in October that food source gets on a ferry and goes home, and the cats do not. So the app explains it briefly, without lecturing, and always ends with something to do instead — Syros Cats feeds colonies properly, all year round, and €1 covers a cat for a day.

What a collaboration would look like

Boundless Life providesAn education contact to shape how it fits a cohort’s week; a cohort group set up at each arrival; a mention in the welcome so families know it exists on day one; and honest feedback on what works with children of different ages.
Syros Cats providesThe expertise behind everything the app teaches, the welfare response when a cohort reports a cat in trouble, and the note at the end of each stay saying what the group’s work led to.
Fair Shake providesThe app itself, in three languages, on iOS and Android, plus the group features, the coverage maps and the end-of-stay summaries — built and operated as a managed service.
Cost to Boundless LifeNothing. The app is free to use, and nothing here asks a family to spend money. Donations exist and are entirely optional, and they are never asked for in front of a child.
Children’s dataThe app is built to know as little about anybody as it can. A spotter is anonymous by default, with no name, no email address and no account, and a child can take part fully that way. Group membership is a joining code, not a profile.

What we would like to talk about

  • Does this fit a cohort’s week as a running project, a one-off field day, or something families simply do on their own time? We have designed for all three, and you know which one works.
  • Which ages the learning strands should be pitched at, and where they need to be simpler or harder.
  • A trial with one cohort, with a neighbourhood assigned and a summary at the end, before anybody commits to more.
  • Whether this travels. Boundless Life runs several sites and the app is not specific to Syros — the same idea works anywhere there are street cats and a local organisation caring for them.
